## What it says

Online focus groups with 146 residents of the Solent region in southern England (cities, suburbs, villages and an island) on what makes a journey with two or more means of transport difficult. The picture is almost wholly negative. For everyone the main barriers are timetables that do not match, the extra time, the risk that one late leg breaks the whole journey, and separate tickets. Rural residents add missing services and unsafe routes to stations; urban and suburban residents add the complexity of planning with many options. People prefer to combine one leg they control with one they do not.

## Data and quotes used

- Method: asynchronous online focus groups over 17 days, nine groups (urban, peri-urban, rural by male, female, mixed), 146 participants (49 urban, 70 peri-urban, 27 rural; mean age 46), recruited mostly through local Facebook groups, 10 pounds each; one open question on the biggest challenges of multi-modal journeys; 182 posts, 19,559 words; thematic analysis, 19 themes, second coder agreement 81% (PDF p.3-6). Funded by the UK transport ministry within a regional programme.
- The four most frequent themes were the same in all places: timetable coordination and coverage first, time second, then 'critical reliability' and ticketing (PDF p.7).
- Timetables: 'My multi mode journeys involve a ferry across the Solent then a train. The timings simply do not work meaning the train often leaves before I arrive on the ferry'; 'the last train runs far too early' (PDF p.7).
- Time includes the buffer people add: the theme covers 'waiting and having to build in wiggle room'; 'walking to the bus stop and getting the bus somewhere adds a considerable amount of time to both the outward and return journey, especially given the unreliability of buses being early or late' (table 2 and PDF p.9).
- Critical reliability: 'the risk that one trip is delayed and then you miss the next part of your journey or is even cancelled and you are left in the middle of nowhere'; 'Anyone who has been caught out by scarce, unreliable, and irregular bus and train connections will conclude that the stress and expense of trying to do the right thing is just not worthwhile' (PDF p.10).
- The 'wait time dilemma', named by six people: 'Having enough time for transition between modes but not so much that it adds a huge amount of journey time or you are left standing in the cold/rain/dark for ages' (PDF p.9, p.11).
- One leg under your control: 'the best way to combine different modes of transport is to have one mode within your control and the other outside of your control. So walking and the train, e-scooter and the train ... That way you are in control of adjusting one mode if the train/bus/ferry is late or delayed. Combining the train and a bus or ferry and the bus are things I would like to avoid' (PDF p.11).
- Ticketing: 'the biggest issues with multi-modal travel is the stacking of costs'; 'I want to go out into town by train as it's quicker but come home on the bus as it runs later ... This means buying two tickets at two different cost (both almost the same as a return fare)'; 'Buying different tickets for different bus services and train services gets complicated and makes it harder to work within a budget' (PDF p.8, p.11).
- Problems multiply: 'Basically any reasons not to use public transport are squared, not doubled, when using multiple'; 'this is where public transport falls down' (PDF p.11).
- Planning is a barrier only where there are options: 'I find it rather complicated navigating multiple websites for each type of transport to find my journey'. No rural participant raised route and time planning; the author concludes that for rural areas 'service provision (rather than information provision) is far more important' (PDF p.9, p.12).
- Getting to the station: unsafe roads for cycling in rural areas, a dark walk at the other end, 'sometimes there is no transport option other than a taxi at the other end', bikes hard to take on trains and at risk of theft at stations (PDF p.11-12).
- A hybrid commuter with a disability: 'We drive to our local station and use blue badge to park in streets nearby as we only commute one or two days a week and the station parking is crazily expensive' (table 2, PDF p.8).
- A fallback given up: 'There was only one bus from my workplace to home ... It took five times longer than driving a car ... this's unfortunately not my option any more, even when the train is cancelled' (PDF p.9).
- Earlier research cited: satisfaction with one public transport leg depends on the legs before it, which supports a 'door-to-door perspective' (Susilo and Cats 2014, PDF p.2).
- Recommendations of the author: mandatory timetable coordination between operators, cross-service ticketing with price caps, joint planning and ticketing for urban and peri-urban areas, safe routes and secure bike parking at stations, shared vehicles and on-demand services to reach public transport in rural areas (table 3, PDF p.14).

## Limits

One region of England that is dispersed and car-dependent, with ferries; people volunteered through Facebook, so those with grievances about transport may be over-represented, as the author says. The question was about challenges, so positive experiences were not asked for. Journeys are not separated by purpose or distance: commuting between cities is not singled out. Counts per theme were in a table that did not convert. Most participants posted once, so it is closer to an open survey than to a discussion.
