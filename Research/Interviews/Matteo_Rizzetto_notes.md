## Who

- Lives in Como, four to six minutes on foot from the Como San Giovanni station. Works in design; the office is in Manno, in Switzerland, near Lugano.
- He describes several commutes: Como to the office in Manno; Como to Milan, where he goes to three or four different places; for a period Como to Turin every two weeks at weekends, by train; and for a while he had a bed in Milan.
- Since the pandemic he goes to the office much less than before. How often he goes now is not said.
- His work depends on the project: "non posso organizzarmi la vita come cazzo mi pare perché [il] lavoro è tutto tranne che una cosa stabile" (EN: I cannot organise my life as I like, because work is anything but stable).

## Journey 1: Como to the office in Manno

### By car, what he does now

- Door to door 45 minutes "se tutto va bene" (EN: if all goes well), with no traffic. He has a garage near home.
- How the car came in: after the pandemic he went to the office about once a week, so he stopped the train pass: "70 euro al mese per andare una volta a settimana" (EN: 70 euro a month to go once a week) was the opposite of what a pass is for. Then he kept driving.
- He says it himself: "semplicemente perché è un'abitudine che mi è rimasta da un momento in cui mi era storicamente più conveniente andare in macchina" (EN: simply because it is a habit left over from a time when driving was more convenient for me).
- What the car gives him: he likes to be "flessibile e indipendente"; it is faster than the train; and it lets him be back in Como when the working day ends, so that he can "avere una vita" (EN: have a life). By train he would be home at about 19:30 with everything still to do. Driving at the end of the working day would take more than an hour.
- Time in the car is lost time for him: "è proprio una tortura per me stare fermo in macchina" (EN: sitting still in a car is real torture for me), because he can do nothing else.
- The car costs him more than the train.

### By train, what he did for years and is thinking of doing again

| Leg | What he said |
| --- | --- |
| Home to the platform | On foot, four to six minutes |
| Como to Lugano | Train at 8:23. It comes from Milan |
| Change at Lugano | A wait of 12 to 14 minutes |
| Lugano to the stop near the office | A second train, one stop |
| Stop to the office | About one kilometre on foot, about ten minutes |
| Whole journey | "dalle 8 e 23 alle 9:30 più o meno", about an hour and twenty minutes with the walk to the station |

- The direct train to the stop near the office no longer exists. He names this as his main pain, and as one he can do nothing about.
- The walk at the end "non mi pesa" (EN: is no burden) except in the rain or in the summer heat: it is all road, with no cover.
- He is looking at the timetables to see whether he can go back to the train, and wants to try it a couple of times. With the current project he may have commitments at lunchtime, so he is not sure.
- The pass for the Italian part, Como to Chiasso, is a paper ticket bought at a kiosk near the station, or online with difficulty. It costs 24 euro for one stop of a few kilometres, which annoys him: almost the price of the Milan underground pass. For the Swiss part there is a company pass.

### When the train fails

- Delays and cancellations happened more often than he liked. He gives the reasons he sees: the trains come from Milan Centrale; there is one line with few alternatives; problems at Chiasso, where the crew sometimes changes.
- His routine: he does not check before leaving. "Forse non guardo neanche, forse vado in stazione, guardo se il treno è cancellato, sospeso, in ritardo, qualsiasi cosa, torno a casa, prendo le chiavi della macchina, vado in garage e vado in macchina" (EN: maybe I do not even look; I go to the station, see whether the train is cancelled or late, go back home, take the car keys, go to the garage and drive).
- Other options he names: wait for the next train, or take the one before, as a colleague does (8:10 against his 8:23): if the earlier one fails, the usual one is still there.
- The change at Lugano is where a delay becomes a missed train. He has learned the threshold: if the delay goes over 12 minutes before Lugano, "io il treno l'ho già perso" (EN: I have already missed the train).
- His fallback at Lugano is a city bus, line 5: four to five minutes on foot from the station, and it stops 150 metres from the office. It takes a little longer. He does not know the bus timetable and does not look it up: "gli orari dei pullman in Svizzera non li conosco, non li so, non li controllo e non mi interessa" (EN: I do not know the Swiss bus times, I do not check them and I do not care). He only knows that if the connection fails he goes to the 5.
- Because he knows the threshold, he can warn the people he is meeting before he arrives.
- On the way back, if the train is cancelled he takes the 5 or waits for the next train, one every half hour.

## Journey 2: Como to Milan

- Nine times out of ten by train. It becomes the car depending on the time of day or when a train is cancelled.
- From Como San Giovanni there are direct trains to Milano Centrale, at times he finds more comfortable: 8:36, which leaves him time for a coffee.
- The train is often full and he expects to stand.
- "Il treno per Milano ha sempre 10 minuti di ritardo, non meno di dieci minuti" (EN: the train to Milan is always 10 minutes late, never less). He plans with that.
- In Milan he does not look at the underground times. From Centrale he counts 20 minutes in the worst case to reach any of the places he goes to.
- When he lived on the other side of Como he took the train from the other station, Como Lago, which arrives at Milano Garibaldi and suited some destinations better.
- The train ride takes from 40 minutes to a little over an hour, depending on the train.
- "Come tutte le routine, io non mi vado mai a controllare" (EN: as with every routine, I never go and check). He sees what is happening when he arrives at the station.
- Trains were cancelled several times. Then "l'unica opzione che avevo era tornare a casa, prendere la macchina" (EN: the only option I had was to go home and take the car), drive to the park and ride at the end of an underground line, where there is always space and the car park costs very little, and take the underground to the centre.

## How he finds out, and when

- At the station, on the display, like the interviewer.
- He checks before leaving only in two cases. When the day matters: an early meeting, a class to teach, something where he must not be late; then he starts checking as he leaves home, and if he is late himself he takes the car keys with him. And when the trip is not routine: leisure trips, a concert, a train to Turin. "Quando non è puro dovere, mi metto a controllare qualche dettaglio" (EN: when it is not pure duty, I check a few more details).
- The cancellation comes late. The delay grows on the display, 5 minutes, then 10, then 15: "a 20 inizio a sentire la puzza, a 25 la puzza si fa più forte e aspetto solo che appaia la scritta annullato" (EN: at 20 I begin to smell it, at 25 the smell is stronger and I only wait for the word cancelled to appear). He counts this as the real pain point: "ti avvisano davvero all'ultimo per i treni cancellati" (EN: they really tell you at the last moment about cancelled trains).
- Several times he gave up and stayed at home, "perché abbiamo un lavoro che ce lo permette" (EN: because we have a job that allows it). Otherwise he drives.
- On a Friday evening at Milano Centrale, going to Turin, a train with 150 minutes of delay: "lì non puoi fare niente se non aspettare" (EN: there you can do nothing but wait).

## Tools and channels

- A third-party timetable app, which he says was later bought by a ticket seller. It searched by route or by train number and showed the actual times, which he found "più veritieri rispetto a quelli di Trenitalia, e rispetto a quelli dei tabelloni" (EN: more truthful than the operator's and than the station boards).
- His explanation, given as his own thought: the operator holds the information back while someone decides. "Non capisco perché un'app non ufficiale te lo possa dire prima, e tu no" (EN: I do not understand why an unofficial app can tell me earlier and you cannot).
- A chat channel among colleagues of the Lugano office, where people warned each other about trouble with the trains. It was used much more when everyone took the train regularly; now someone still writes now and then.
- The operator's app, which he opens but does not describe using for alerts.
- A friend's set-up, told as an example. The friend commutes by one train line with no alternative, and ten minutes of delay have to be reported at work as leave. He has set the operator's app to notify him only on the three days he goes to the office: one notification on the state of the line when he wakes up, and others timed for the seven minutes he drives to the station, so that he can wait in the warm car if the train is late.

## What he would change

- "Sarebbe molto utile sapere delle cose con anticipo" (EN: it would be very useful to know things in advance): if works start tonight on the line he takes tomorrow morning, tell him now.
- The operator could know that he is a regular: he always buys the same ticket or pass. He would like to be treated differently from an occasional traveller, "mia zia che compra il biglietto per andare in vacanza" (EN: my aunt who buys a ticket to go on holiday), and would even tell them himself that he makes this trip often.
- With the paper pass the Italian operator has no way to reach him; the Swiss one has his contact details.
- He has no interest in news once he is in Switzerland: by then he is there, and he knows what to do.
- He adds that such notices may already exist and that he misses them, because he hardly looks at notifications.
- Notifications he does look at: one-to-one chats and a few groups on one messaging app, text messages, and delivery apps. "Io odio le notifiche che non siano tali" (EN: I hate notifications that are not real ones). A second messaging app is muted and used on demand; he thinks its channels could work for other people.
- On pain in general: "ci diciamo che è una roba che dobbiamo fare, quindi uno trova il modo di sormontare i pain importanti" (EN: we tell ourselves it is something we have to do, so one finds a way over the big pains).

## Findings (agent's reading)

One interview confirms nothing; it shows where to look.

- **A pass that no longer fits started the move to the car, and habit kept it.** He stopped the pass when the office days fell to one a week, and names habit himself as the reason he still drives.
- **He decides without opening a tool, and checks only when the day is not routine.** New compared with the desk research: the trigger for checking is the importance of the day, not the trip.
- **The cost of the train is time at the home end, not the journey itself.** With the car he is home when work ends. This is a constraint (Q4) the desk research did not have.
- **A learned threshold and a fallback known by heart.** Twelve minutes of delay before Lugano, then bus line 5, with no timetable looked up. The fallback for the first leg is his own car.
- **The cancellation announced step by step.** He reads the growing delay as a sign and trusts a third-party app more than the operator.
- **The paper pass cuts the operator off from its most regular customers.** This is a reason for the missing early warning that the desk research did not find (Q27, Q30).
- **The last leg on foot is accepted but depends on the weather.**
- **Against the brief:** he does not ask for one place with everything. He asks to be told early about his own line, and he says the main pain, the lost direct train, is a matter of service.

## Not covered

- Tickets and refunds for the Milan trips.
