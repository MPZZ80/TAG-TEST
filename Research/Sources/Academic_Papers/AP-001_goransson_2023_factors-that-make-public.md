## What it says

A review of 19 international literature reviews and 35 Nordic studies on what makes public transport attractive and what moves people from the car to it. Reliability and frequency matter most for demand and satisfaction, but how much demand grows when they improve is uncertain. Travellers dislike walking, waiting and changing more than time on board, and unexpected delays more than expected waits. Car users are not one group and already have reliability and flexibility, so good public transport alone does not move them; life events are the moments when habits can change.

## Data and quotes used

- Method: search in August 2022 in Web of Science, Scopus and Transport Reviews; 190 results screened to 19 international reviews (each covering 20-130 studies); 336 Nordic results screened to 35 studies, all quantitative, only two with in-depth interviews (PDF p.2-4).
- Rule of thumb: walking and waiting time weigh about twice as much as in-vehicle time for non-business trips; one review found waiting from 1.5 to 12 times in-vehicle time. Quote: 'travellers dislike walking, waiting and transferring more than in-vehicle time'; 'PT users have a greater disutility for unexpected and unpredictable delays compared to expected and predictable waits'; the burden of a change is higher than that of waiting (PDF p.4).
- The cost of a change depends on the station: in Greater Copenhagen (4,810 observed trips) one change was worth 5 minutes of in-vehicle time for the best case (easy wayfinding, shops, escalators) and 12 minutes for the worst; the overall range was 3.5 to 30 minutes. Few observed routes had waits over ten minutes per change (PDF p.6).
- Helsinki: two changes weigh more than twice one change; standing weighs more than one change but less than two (PDF p.6). Sweden: standing in a crowd multiplies the value of travel time by 2.9 against sitting with little crowding (PDF p.4).
- Regional trips: important attributes are reliability, frequency, comfort, travel time and network coverage; frequency and reliability weigh somewhat less and comfort more as distance grows; for regional public transport comfort is more important than frequency; station facilities matter more than on-board comfort for regional trips under 25 km (PDF p.6).
- Swedish train passengers (554): moving the departure time is valued less than in-vehicle time (multipliers 0.1-0.7); the least flexible are morning travellers, business travellers, people 45 or older, parents (PDF p.6).
- Reliability and demand: in the Oslo area a 1% rise in average train delay cut demand by 0.04-0.1%, lower than stated-preference estimates; 'an improvement in reliability alone will not lead to a large increase in PT demand' (PDF p.7).
- First and last mile: an automated shuttle between the stop and a Danish campus had no overall effect on public transport share; it moved some existing users to the shuttle (PDF p.7). In a Stockholm trial frequency drove the intention to try the shuttle and comfort the intention to keep using it (PDF p.10).
- Satisfaction: frequent users are more satisfied; passengers who travel longer distances are less satisfied than those on short distances (Sweden); people who actively use mobile devices on board expect productive travel time and are the least satisfied group (Norway); the ability to work on board was rated low in importance by boat commuters (PDF p.8).
- Sweden: frequency, reliability and travel time matter more than having many suitable lines, 'a higher preference for direct, punctual and frequent lines over many low-frequency lines that minimize transfers' (PDF p.8).
- Life events (child birth, moving, retirement) interrupt habits and are a chance to change mode (PDF p.8). People with many daily activities or little flexibility on arrival time at work are more likely to use the car or the bike and less likely to use public transport (Denmark) (PDF p.9). Having a person in care lowers the chance of commuting by bus (Norway) (PDF p.9).
- Distance: the chance of commuting by public transport grows with the distance between home and work (Norway, Sweden); for trips over 25 km trains and buses are the main competitors of the car (Denmark); more use when people live five minutes or less from a stop served at least every 20 minutes (PDF p.9-10).
- MaaS trial in Gothenburg (151 people, six months): 42% reported a change in mode choice, 36% no change; participants could not predict how their own behaviour would change (PDF p.10).
- Car users: 'Since car users already have these qualities in their current travel mode, a PT system with high punctuality and high frequency is not enough to create a mode shift' (PDF p.11). More changes required in public transport raise the chance of going by car; difficulty in parking raises public transport use (PDF p.11). Campaigns do not move 'devoted drivers' (PDF p.10).
- Travellers judge the ticket price against what they expect for the service; public transport users tend to overestimate their waiting time at stops (PDF p.7).

## Limits

A review of reviews: figures come from single studies cited in it and were not checked in the originals. Half of the Nordic evidence is from Sweden; Nordic countries have high public transport use and strong services, so transfer to Italy is uncertain. Mostly urban and regional public transport; little on combined door-to-door trips between cities. The authors say themselves that the effect of reliability and frequency on demand needs more research and that only one study tested whether preferences are stable between places.
