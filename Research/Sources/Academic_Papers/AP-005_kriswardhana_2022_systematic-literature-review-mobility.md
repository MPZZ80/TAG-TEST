## What it says

A systematic review of 29 peer-reviewed studies, published up to April 2022, on who says they would adopt a combined mobility service and how the packages offered to them are built. Almost all of it is stated preference: people choosing among hypothetical packages. Only two studies observe real use. How people travel today predicts interest better than age, sex or income, whose effects contradict each other from study to study. Commuters are a market segment in 2 of the 14 package studies, and trains are among the modes least often included.

## Data and quotes used

- Method: Web of Science and Scopus, English peer-reviewed journals only, 2010 to April 2022, five keyword combinations, 29 papers kept after screening abstracts; grey literature and conference papers excluded (PDF p.2).
- Stated against observed: of the studies in table 1, two rest on real behaviour (the UbiGo trial in Gothenburg, 195 participants; a Sydney trial, 150 participants) and the rest on hypothetical choices, with samples from 161 to 6,405 (table 1, PDF p.3).
- Where: Europe and Australia, a few Asian cities, none in the Americas or Africa (PDF p.9).
- Personal characteristics do not give a stable picture: 'the findings on the influence of socio-demographic characteristics are diverse'. Younger and more educated people are more interested in most studies; sex and income point in opposite directions in different studies; people who work from home are less interested in one Dutch study (PDF p.4-5).
- Travel habits predict better: 'Travel habits and attitudes towards transport modes are found to be stronger predictors for MaaS uptake preferences than socio-demographic characteristics'. People who already combine modes or use public transport often are more interested; frequent car users less (PDF p.5-6).
- Satisfied users see no need: one study across European countries finds that people who travel by public transport and other services 'are not interested in using MaaS, since they are satisfied with their current transport opportunities' (PDF p.6, citing Matowicki et al. 2022).
- Commute length: contradictory. One study finds longer travel times reduce willingness to buy a package, another that people with short commutes are less interested, a third no effect of distance (PDF p.6).
- People living far from a railway station want car sharing and e-bike sharing in the package; for commuters, unlimited train travel to the work region raises the chance of choosing a package, most of all with car sharing (PDF p.6, p.8, citing Farahmand et al. 2021, the Netherlands, 236 respondents).
- Park and ride is valued more inside a package than alone in a Swiss study (PDF p.8, citing Guidon et al. 2020).
- Ease of use counts little in stated intention, and perceived risk counts against: 'the process of MaaS must be simple, clear, and easy to understand' (PDF p.6).
- What the packages contain in the 14 studies that build one: public transport and car sharing in all 14, taxi in 12; train in 4, coach in 1. 'The least popular transport modes included in the mobility bundles are train, coach, and ferry'. Commuters are the target segment in 2 of 14, individual local residents in 11 (table 4 and text, PDF p.7-8).
- Subscription or pay per use: mixed. A Finnish study finds pay per use preferred to price bundles; a Dutch one a preference for plans of 6 to 12 months; pay per use on one platform 'is not attractive enough unless a discount is offered' (PDF p.8).
- Willingness to pay for unlimited monthly public transport inside a package: 110 to 146 euro in five studies, close to the real price; for the other modes it is 'significantly lower than the market prices' (table 5, PDF p.8-10). A Finnish study finds people would pay about half of what they spend on travel now (PDF p.8).
- What is missing from this literature, according to the authors: travel time, cost, waiting time, parking and access to public transport are rarely among the variables; 'factors related to it should be evaluated, e.g., the reliability of the PT system'; real-use data; low-density areas; the difference between people who have a choice and people who do not (PDF p.6, p.9).
- Why car users stay out: 'the intermodal options require longer walking distance and more transfers' (PDF p.10, citing a 2021 study).

## Limits

A review of what people say they would choose when shown a description, not of what they do: 27 of the 29 studies are hypothetical. It covers adoption of a product and the design of its packages, so it starts from a solution and not from travellers' problems. English journals only. The studies are urban; intercity commuting does not appear, and trains are in 4 of 14 package designs. The effects reported are listed study by study without weighing their quality. Table 4 could not be read reliably in the conversion; the counts above are from the text.
