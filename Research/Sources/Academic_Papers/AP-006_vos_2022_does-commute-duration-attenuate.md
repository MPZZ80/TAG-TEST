## What it says

A survey of 1,430 working residents of Ghent who had recently moved home. How long the commute takes explains satisfaction with it better than the means of travel does. Train users are the least satisfied group, and that is because their commutes are the longest: once duration is held constant, using the train or the bus makes no difference. Only frequent car use keeps a negative effect of its own. The authors conclude that public transport is not less satisfying in itself and that the lever is the length, or the perceived length, of the trip.

## Data and quotes used

- Method: online survey in February 2017 sent to all 9,979 households that had moved in 2015-2016 into selected urban and suburban neighbourhoods of Ghent; 1,650 answers (16.5%), 1,430 workers and students used. Satisfaction is the mean of six statements on a five-point scale. More than half of the sample is under 30 and 80% have a degree (PDF p.2-3).
- Commute duration, as the average time the respondent reports to the main workplace: up to 30 minutes for 65.9%, 31 to 60 minutes for 22.5%, over 60 minutes for 11.6% (PDF p.3).
- Train users have the long commutes: 73.4% of those who mostly or always take the train travel more than 45 minutes; 80.1% of those who never take it travel up to 30 minutes (PDF p.4).
- Average satisfaction by duration, on a scale of 1 to 5: 4.41 up to 10 minutes, 3.89 for 10-20, 3.52 for 20-30, 3.01 for 30-45, 2.60 over 45 minutes. The decline is close to linear (table 4, PDF p.6; p.4).
- Average satisfaction by use of the train: 3.77 never, 3.17 mostly, 2.78 always. By car: 3.82 never, 3.14 always. By bicycle: 3.29 never, 3.88 always (table 4, PDF p.6).
- Five groups of commuters: 'long train users' (216 people, 95% with a degree, 87% living in the urban neighbourhoods, probably commuting to Brussels and Antwerp) have the lowest satisfaction, 2.74; car users with medium durations 3.18; bus and tram users 3.37; short active travellers 4.23; short cyclists 4.21 (tables 3 and 4, PDF p.5-6; footnote 5, p.4). The train users also walk and cycle often, 'probably to travel to and from the railway station' (PDF p.4).
- Duration alone explains 38% of the variance in satisfaction, mode use alone 26%, both together 45%. With duration in the model the effect of mostly or always taking the train falls from -1.22 to -0.05 and is no longer significant; frequent car use stays at -0.60 (table 5, PDF p.7).
- Within each duration band only frequent car use keeps a negative effect, except for commutes over 45 minutes (table 6, PDF p.8).
- The authors' conclusion: 'there is no reason to assume that public transport use is inherently less satisfying'; measures 'should not focus on a modal shift away from public transport, but on decreasing (perceived) travel time of public transport trips', also by making trips 'more comfortable, productive, or enjoyable' (PDF p.1, p.8).
- They read the car's own negative effect as congestion at peak hours, citing another study (PDF p.8).
- Their own caveat: other studies did find effects of the mode after controlling for duration, so more studies are needed (PDF p.9).

## Limits

One Belgian city and a sample of people who had just moved home: young and highly educated, not representative. Duration is one self-reported average for the whole commute, not split by leg, so nothing on access, waiting or changes. Attitudes towards the modes are left out, as the authors say. The intercity commuters are only one of the five groups (216 people) and are inferred, not identified. Data from 2017. Cross-section.
