## What it says

A survey of 271 people who commute by bus or train from two suburban cities into Dhaka, on how they reach the stop at home and how they get from the stop to work. The setting is outside the scope: rickshaws and informal shared vehicles carry most of these trips. One pattern transfers as a mechanism and matches other sources in this research: the leg at the work end is longer than the leg at the home end, most of all for train users, and time spent waiting and changing weighs more than time spent moving.

## Data and quotes used

- Method: face-to-face questionnaire at 11 rail stations and bus terminals and on board, December 2016 to January 2017, weekday peaks; 39% of those approached took part; 271 complete answers (152 bus, 119 rail, with rail deliberately over-sampled); regular work commuters from Narayanganj and Gazipur who have both bus and rail available. Non-random sample, as the authors state (PDF p.3-5).
- Home end against work end: average distance 1.25 km to the stop and 2.39 km from the stop to the destination; waiting 4.20 against 5.56 minutes; time in the vehicle 12.44 against 21.36 minutes (table 2, PDF p.6).
- Train against bus: rail users travel 1.35 km to the station and 2.68 km from it; bus users 1.14 and 2.09 km. In-vehicle time at the work end is 23.34 minutes for rail and 19.34 for bus (table 3, PDF p.6).
- The authors on why: 'employment centers and trip attraction points are dispersed across the city while only a few transit routes directly connect these places'; the rail catchment is larger because stations are fewer (PDF p.5).
- Waiting and changing weigh more than riding: 'additional time for waiting and transfer for the vehicles contributes to more disutility than does travel time for the same duration' (PDF p.8; tables 6 and 7, p.9-10).
- Rail users are much more likely to need a motorised vehicle at the work end (coefficient 2.35 in the simple model, significant), while the main mode makes no difference at the home end (tables 6 and 7, PDF p.9-10).
- The authors' conclusion: 'the weakest part of commuter rail is the last mile connectivity'; feeder services are 'fragmented and disorganized', with no stops for them at or near stations, so commuters walk far to change (PDF p.10).
- Being able to find the vehicle counts: ease of finding the mode near the stop is a significant factor in the choice, and comfort counts more at the home end than at the work end, where people 'tend to compromise comfort' (PDF p.9).
- From the literature it reviews: the first and last mile 'deters transit ridership, even when high quality service is provided by the main system', above all on longer trips; on long trips the weight of access time is smaller; in the Netherlands having a car does not change how people reach the station, while in the United States it makes driving there more likely (PDF p.1-2).
- The authors' own caveat: one typical day was recorded, and 'mode choice can vary from day to day', as can the outward and return trip (PDF p.10-11).

## Limits

Bangladesh: a city where 5% of trips are by private car and rickshaws carry about a third; the modes, incomes and services are not comparable with the countries in scope, so only the pattern of a longer and heavier work-end leg is usable, and only as a mechanism. Suburban commuting into one metropolis, not between cities. A convenience sample caught at stops, two thirds men. Data from 2016-2017. Nothing on information, planning, tickets or disruption.
