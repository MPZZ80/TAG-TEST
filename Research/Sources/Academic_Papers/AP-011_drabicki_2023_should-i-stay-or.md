## What it says

A stated-choice survey of 377 passengers waiting at bus and tram stops in Krakow: shown how crowded the vehicle due now and the next one are, would they board or wait? Half to three quarters would let a severely overcrowded vehicle go; one in eight to one in three would skip a moderately crowded one. Having to arrive on time halves the willingness to wait, and regular users of the line wait less than occasional ones. It is evidence about a kind of information that commuters do not have today, on short urban trips with a vehicle every 5 to 10 minutes.

## Data and quotes used

- Method: face-to-face stated-preference survey on tablets at 8 inner-city bus and tram stops in Krakow, March and April 2019; 377 valid questionnaires, response rate about 50%; six choice scenarios each, set in the respondent's own current trip; waiting time for the second vehicle 5 or 10 minutes; mixed logit models. About 75% of respondents are under 40 (PDF p.3-6).
- A four-level crowding scale tested in focus groups: more than half the seats free; some seats free; all seats taken with room to stand; severely overcrowded with a risk of not getting on. At level 4 people 'unless in a hurry' would 'consider different travel options' (table 1, PDF p.4).
- First vehicle moderately crowded, next one with seats: 30% would wait 5 minutes and 12% would wait 10 minutes (PDF p.6, p.11).
- First vehicle severely overcrowded: about 75% would wait 5 minutes and 45% would wait 10 minutes. Offering seats on the second vehicle instead of standing room adds only 1 to 3 points: 'urban PT users place higher emphasis on avoiding the risks of excessive overcrowding in the first place ... while seat availability seems to be of secondary importance' (PDF p.3, p.6, p.11).
- Having to arrive on time: with a severely overcrowded first vehicle, 43% of those who must arrive on time board it anyway against 15% of those who need not; with moderate crowding 84% against 60% (table 3, PDF p.7). Each minute of waiting weighs about twice as much on a time-critical trip (PDF p.8).
- Trip purpose: from home to work 79% board a moderately crowded vehicle and 46% a severely overcrowded one; from work to home the figures are 74% and 15%. People wait more readily on the way home (table 3, PDF p.7; p.6).
- Regular users wait less: among those who use the route 5 to 7 days a week 31% board the severely overcrowded vehicle, against 13% of those who use it once a week. The authors' explanation: they 'may become eventually more accustomed to the travel discomfort' (table 3, PDF p.7; p.6).
- Age: of respondents over 65, 58% would wait 10 minutes even to avoid moderate crowding and 89% to avoid severe overcrowding; under 25 the figures are 4% and 41% (table 3, PDF p.7).
- Acceptable wait, from the model: on average 2 to 4 minutes to avoid moderate crowding and 6 to 12 minutes to avoid severe overcrowding, the higher values for trips with no fixed arrival time; up to 20-25 minutes in individual cases (PDF p.9, p.11).
- Comparison the authors draw with a British study of rail commuters (Preston et al. 2017): there the acceptable wait for a train with seats is 8 to 23 minutes, and seat availability and working on board count for more on longer regional trips (PDF p.2, p.11).
- From the literature it reviews: being unable to board weighs up to 3.5 times as much as ordinary waiting; standing in a crowd multiplies perceived travel time by about 1.5 to 2.5; overcrowding raises the feeling that the arrival time is unreliable, and this is made worse when accurate and timely information is missing (PDF p.2).
- State of crowding information in 2021-2023 as the authors describe it: 'still in their early practical deployment stages', limited to carriage loads on some railways (London, Stockholm, Sydney, Tokyo, the Dutch railways), bus loads in Seoul and Singapore, and estimates in apps such as Moovit, Google Maps and JakDojade based on past user reports (PDF p.2).
- The authors' caveats: stated preference 'is prone to overestimation' of how much people value avoiding crowds; simulations report 'inaccuracy risks of instantaneous crowding information'; such a system works only if the information is trustworthy (PDF p.11-12).

## Limits

Hypothetical choices, which the authors themselves say tend to overstate; nobody was observed waiting. Urban bus and tram trips of about 20 minutes with a vehicle every 5 to 10 minutes: on an intercity service with one train every 30 or 60 minutes the trade is of a different order, and the paper does not test it. One Polish city, 377 people, three quarters under 40, unweighted. Only two waiting times were offered. Respondents were told to treat the crowding information as certain. Data from 2019, before the pandemic.
