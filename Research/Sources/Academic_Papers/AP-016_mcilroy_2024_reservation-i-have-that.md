## What it says

The second paper from the same online focus groups (146 residents of the Solent region in England), on what they think of a journey planning and ticketing app about to be launched there. Accurate, reliable and live information is the core requirement for everyone. The strongest message is that an app will not get people out of their cars unless the services improve: it is 'the icing on an imaginary cake'. Rural residents say they would not use it because there is nothing to plan; suburban residents are the most sceptical and the most interested in live information, parking and facilities at stations; one bad experience would end the trust.

## Data and quotes used

- Method: same nine asynchronous online focus groups as AP-015 (146 people, 17 days, Solent region with three cities, suburbs, villages and an island). Participants saw a definition of MaaS and a promotional video of the regional app, then two questions: what incentives would make them use it and how it might fail; what information is most and least useful. 16,278 and 14,448 words; 541 and 568 coded segments; second-coder agreement 80% and 91% (PDF p.3-6).
- The concern named most in all places: 'a reservation I have is that presumably no travel app will improve the actual services. Serious improvements must be made to reliability and frequency in order to truly improve public transport' (woman, 37, urban). 'The infrastructure and services seem to be the issue, not the ease of booking them' (man, 45). The app is 'the icing on an imaginary cake. We need a decent cake' (woman, 61, peri-urban) (PDF p.8, p.11).
- Author's conclusion: 'a journey planning and ticketing app is a facilitator not a driver of travel behaviour change' (PDF p.13).
- The most frequent requirement, in all groups: live, reliable, accurate, joined-up service information. 'I think the absolutely most fundamental thing about the success of the app will be whether it's reliable. Does it tell you the right information?' (man, 37). The author notes a perception that existing public transport data are not reliable, and that the system 'will only be as good as the data on which it is based' (PDF p.8, p.11).
- It has to beat what people already use: 'I would have to be confident that it was a better alternative to using a combination of Google (for route planning) and transport providers' own websites/apps' (man, 54). 'You can already do something like this with trains and busses on Google' (PDF p.7, p.14).
- Price must be the best: 'I would not be convinced that it offered the cheapest ticket price therefore would then compare the app to buying separately which would take longer'; 'I absolutely do not trust app services which are not run directly by the company I am trying to buy the service from' (table A1, PDF p.15).
- One bad experience: 'One bad experience (i.e., not real time info leading to missing a connection or realising you could have got your ticket elsewhere) will stop people using it and it will be very hard to rebuild trust in the system once it is lost' (table A1, PDF p.17).
- Who is responsible when a connection is missed: 'If for example it was a train that was missed who would you then go to for compensation for delays etc. Would this be the train provider or breeze?' (table A1, PDF p.17). A wished feature: 'a link to the respective agency ... to clarify that my missing the ferry was not my fault' (table A2, PDF p.21).
- Resilience asked of the tool: 'If I was to change my plans halfway through my journey, could the app accommodate for this? Or can the app adapt to delays?' (PDF p.16). Wished information on 'impacts and alternatives': 'if a bus/train is missed, what would the implications be? (is there another service in 10 min, 1 h...)' (table A2, PDF p.21). Push messages for delays and changes (PDF p.22).
- Other information asked for: live position of the vehicle (most prominent among suburban residents); total journey time and cost compared with fuel and parking ('I add up all the costs usually (tickets + car park) vs taking my car'); car parking at stations, its availability and price (suburban and rural residents); whether there is staff ('Is there a human being on site at all in case there are any problems. How would I find out about delays / cancellations'); the historic reliability of a service; saved journeys and journey history; working backwards from a required arrival time (PDF p.9-10, table A2).
- Least useful information: health effects, environmental effect ('in most cases there is no option to choose a greener alternative'), cleanliness, adverts (PDF p.10, p.22).
- By place: urban residents were mostly positive and sceptical only about details; rural residents would simply not use it ('There is not enough modes of transport in my area to bother with it'); suburban residents made almost as many negative as positive comments and qualified the positive ones ('I'd use it if...'). The author sees suburban commuters as the group where such a tool could matter most (PDF p.7, p.11-13).
- A history of failed regional apps feeds scepticism: 'We had solent go which just seemed to fade away. Why will any other apps be any better!' (man, 56) (PDF p.7).
- Concern for people without a smartphone or with an empty battery, raised in all groups (PDF p.8-9, p.17).
- Incentives were among the least discussed themes although the question asked for them: 'a well-functioning app requires no further incentive' in the author's reading; discounts and loyalty schemes were the suggestions (PDF p.8).

## Limits

Same sample and limits as AP-015: one car-dependent English region, volunteers recruited through Facebook, mostly one post each. People reacted to a description and a promotional video of an app they had not used, so these are expectations, not experience. The app offered only planning and ticketing, with no subscription. Not about commuting between cities specifically. Counts by sub-theme are in tables that converted badly.
