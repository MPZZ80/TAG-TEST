## What it says

The evaluation of UbiGo, a six-month trial in Gothenburg in 2013-2014 in which 83 households, 195 people, paid a monthly subscription for public transport, car sharing, rental car, taxi and bike sharing through one app. It is one of the two studies in this field that observe paying customers and not hypothetical choices. People were satisfied and drove less than they had expected. The authors trace the change less to having everything in one place than to three design features: paying only for what is used, a daily ticket that removed zones and time limits, and an app that put every option on the same list each time. Participants turned out not to know their own travel needs when they joined.

## Data and quotes used

- Method: field trial of six months, November 2013 to April 2014, with real customers paying at least 1,200 SEK a month (about 135 euro) per household; 83 subscriptions, 195 people (173 adults); three questionnaires (151 adults answered all three), 14 individual and 3 household interviews of 60 to 90 minutes; no control group and no random selection; 20 households set their car aside for the trial with compensation (PDF p.3-6).
- Who joined: self-selected urban households near public transport and car sharing; 42% lived in the city centre (23% of the municipality), 76% had more than three years of post-secondary education, 80% were employed, 1% retired; 52% of households had no car (table 1, PDF p.4-5; p.12).
- Why they joined: 63% gave curiosity as the main reason, 'with convenience/flexibility, economy, and environment far behind' (PDF p.6).
- Satisfaction: 93% were satisfied with the service, 69% more satisfied with their travel than before, 79% wanted to continue and another 18% on conditions about price and the reliability of the technology. Quote after the trial ended: 'it is a bother to travel in the normal way' (PDF p.6).
- Four groups among the 151: Car shedders (19), who wanted to try living without their car; Car accessors (46), who wanted a car without buying one; Simplifiers (34), who already used several services and wanted 'a smarter way of accessing' them through 'one stop access'; Economizers (52), regular public transport users who wanted it cheaper (table 2, PDF p.8).
- What changed, as reported at the end: mode for 42%, amount of planning before a trip for 34%, chaining of trips 21%, destinations 21%, travel time 21%; 36% reported no change, mainly Simplifiers and Car accessors (PDF p.9).
- Less car than expected: 95% of Car shedders and 53% of Economizers used their car considerably less. Quote: 'We had heaps of car credits left. I approximated for a greater need that wasn't there in the end' (PDF p.9).
- The car at the door: 'You notice if the car is sitting out there, then travel behavior changes' (PDF p.9). People without a car who thought they needed one 'discovered that they managed well without' (PDF p.1, p.10).
- More planning, and not resented: 'Car driving has gone down because we have planned more. Yes, you coordinate more and merge trips... sometimes you don't go'; participants 'did not consider this increased planning as something negative' (PDF p.10).
- Pay for what you use against the monthly pass: 'It doesn't cost any money if you don't use public transport one day, which is a big difference from a monthly pass of course. It matters if you are at home for 3 days and get to pay for those days anyhow' (PDF p.11).
- The pass and the owned car lock people in: 'Owning a car costs a considerable amount of money even if one does not use it and therefore one might as well use it; a PT pass creates similar effects'. In the trial the lock-in shrank to a day: people used public transport more on days when they had already activated the daily ticket (PDF p.13).
- The daily ticket simplified public transport: 'one did not have to keep track of how many minutes it was valid, choosing zones was made much easier, and one did not have to tap in and out' (PDF p.11).
- The list in the app worked against habit: every option shown 'with equal weight' each time 'appeared to enforce the idea that one had to make a choice every time'. Quote: 'this was just the right level of resistance for me to see which mode is good for me today' (PDF p.11-12). The authors call it 'a kind of habit intervenor' (PDF p.14).
- People did not know their own needs: 'the participants were unaware of their preconditions, travel needs, and behavior when joining the service, and thereby could not predict how their behavior would change'; being able to change the subscription each month let them correct it (PDF p.14).
- A gap in the offer: neither car sharing nor rental fitted day trips with a long stay at the destination. Quote: 'I need [the car] only in the morning, but then it will just sit there for 8 h with the costing more and more every hour' (PDF p.10).
- The authors' own reading of what mattered: elements 'deeper than the general MaaS concept of bundling or having everything in one spot', namely the way one pays, the perceived freedom to choose and the way choices are presented (PDF p.13).
- Their caveat: 'it is difficult to draw any general conclusions regarding how many people will change behavior due to a service of this type' (PDF p.14).

## Limits

Eighty-three self-selected households in one Swedish city, most of them central, highly educated and curious about the idea; no control group; behaviour change is self-reported. Urban trips inside one city: no intercity travel and nothing specific to commuting. The service ran in 2013-2014 as a project with a customer service desk open around the clock, and stopped when the trial ended. Nothing on live information or disruption. The authors work in the group that designed and ran the trial.
