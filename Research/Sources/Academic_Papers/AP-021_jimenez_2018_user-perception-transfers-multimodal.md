## What it says

Five focus groups (30 people) in Madrid and Vitoria-Gasteiz on how people experience changes between public transport services inside the city. Time spent walking and waiting is the main cost of a change; real-time information makes the wait feel shorter. Two new costs appear that do not depend on time: the mental effort of staying alert so as not to miss the stop, and the interruption of what people were doing on board. A later survey by the same team put the pure cost of a change at 15-18 minutes of in-vehicle time.

## Data and quotes used

- Method: 5 focus groups, March-September 2015, 80-90 minutes each, in Spanish; 30 participants (Madrid: 8 young adults, 7 adult workers, 5 over 65; Vitoria: 6 under 65, 4 over 65); recruited by e-mail with very low response (for example 2,428 e-mails, 11 answers); thematic analysis; 10-euro voucher (PDF p.5-7).
- Time is the first factor, named by almost all. ES-origin quotes given in English in the paper: 'The worst thing about transfers is the time' (woman, 46, Vitoria); 'A 5-minute transfer in a 15-minute trip is not the same as a 10-minute transfer in a 60-minute trip' (woman, 25): change time as a share of total time is a new factor (PDF p.8).
- Real-time information came second in all groups: 'When you know the waiting time for the next train or bus, waiting becomes more enjoyable and relaxing' (woman, 27); 'I use a mobile phone app a lot. I consider those minutes as saved time' (woman, 21). Young people check waiting times on the phone while travelling and even decide where to change; older people prefer panels at the stop (PDF p.8).
- People use real-time information to decide on the spot: 'If I ... see I have to wait five minutes for the next bus, then I make the transfer, but if I see I have to wait ten minutes I don't, because it only takes me ten minutes to get home on foot' (man, 26, Vitoria) (PDF p.8).
- Mental effort (new factor): 'I'd rather sit on the bus than have to be thinking two stops until I have to get off. If I fall asleep I'll miss my stop' (woman, 25, Madrid) (PDF p.10).
- Activity disruption (new factor, Madrid, longer trips): 'I prefer a longer trip without any transfers so I can read or study, to a shorter trip with transfers because it interrupts whatever activity you're doing ... I take the longest route with the fewest possible transfers, even though it takes longer' (man, 21). A change interrupts the activity 'even if the transfer is ideal (no walking and without waiting time)' (PDF p.10).
- Change between different modes is the worst: 'Bus stops are usually some distance from the metro station entrances; metro-bus transfers are not an option because the networks aren't coordinated' (man, 32, Madrid); 'The bus stops are sometimes poorly signposted so you can't see them when you come out of the metro. So metro-bus is the worst transfer' (woman, 53, Madrid) (PDF p.9).
- Familiarity: 'it is important to have the map in your head' (man, 21, Madrid); an older woman follows a companion who 'knows where all the elevators are' (PDF p.8). Unfamiliarity raises uncertainty and may deter people from routes with changes (PDF p.7).
- Coordination: 'It would be nice if bus drivers would wait when they see another bus approaching the stop so the passengers can transfer' (man, 71, Vitoria); missed connections cause anxiety (PDF p.10).
- Almost all Madrid participants avoid some metro changes because the platforms are too deep, even without mobility problems; over 65s avoid stairs, escalators and long corridors (PDF p.8-9). Crowding matters in Madrid (PDF p.9).
- Follow-up stated-preference survey in Madrid by the same team (cited, not read here): cost for commuters in equivalent in-vehicle minutes: crowding +3.6, change between different modes +2.2, waiting +1.1 per minute, walking +0.8; the pure cost of a change is 15.2-17.7 minutes (PDF p.11).
- Literature cited: a bus-rail change costs nearly twice a rail-rail change; a same-platform change costs less than one with a change of level; crowding doubles the cost of a minute in a station; good information and reliability raise the feeling of control and the intention to use routes with changes (PDF p.2, 4).
- Context: in Madrid 9% of public transport trips are intermodal; in Vitoria 0.45% of all trips (PDF p.5).

## Limits

Urban changes only: the authors say the results 'may not be extrapolated' to changes between cities. Small groups (4-8 people); two of the three Madrid groups were recruited at one university. Fieldwork in 2015. Quotes are the authors' English translations of Spanish speech, not the original words. The factor table from the literature did not convert and was not read. The minute values come from a later paper that was not read.
