## What it says

A laboratory experiment on car routes: 496 people chose among three routes drawn on a map of Lyon, 5,535 choices in all, most of them with the travel time of each route shown. Even with the times in front of them, people took the fastest route six times out of ten. One participant in ten always took the fastest. The average participant treated routes up to about 30% slower than the fastest as equally acceptable and then chose among them on other grounds, above all how direct the route looks. People weigh a time difference in proportion to the length of the trip, not in minutes. Labelling a route as the fastest raises its chances by itself.

## Data and quotes used

- Method: six computer experiments between February 2018 and February 2019 on a web platform showing the Lyon road network; 41 origin-destination pairs with three routes each; 496 participants, 66% students of traffic courses in Lyon and 34% staff of a transport research institute and other universities invited by e-mail; 5,535 choices, 11.2 per person on average; 353 participants (71%) were shown estimated travel times for the three routes, the others only the map. Car routes only. Participants were told to pick the route they considered best 'to complete a trip on time' (PDF p.2-4).
- With the travel times shown, the fastest route was chosen in 60.5% of choices, the second fastest in 23.6% and the slowest in 15.9%. Without times: 46.0%, 32.8% and 21.2% (table 2, PDF p.9).
- Only 9.5% of informed participants chose the fastest route every time; the share of fastest-route choices per person averages 0.58 and is spread 'more or less' evenly between 0.20 and 1.0 (PDF p.9).
- By origin-destination pair, the share choosing the fastest route ranges from 0.27 to 0.92; it is highest where the fastest route is also the one with most motorway (PDF p.9).
- Relative, not absolute: the share choosing the fastest route rises steadily with the percentage difference from the second fastest, and exceeds 0.90 for the largest differences; against the difference in minutes it shows no steady rise. 'Travellers evaluate relative rather than absolute differences in travel time': 5 minutes weigh more on a 10-minute trip than on a 30-minute one (PDF p.9-10, p.16).
- When the fastest route is at least about 30% faster than the others, it is chosen in about 80% of cases (abstract, PDF p.1); at a 35% difference the estimate is 82% (PDF p.12).
- The indifference band: the average participant 'was indifferent to relative travel time differences of less than 31%'; at least 70% of participants would not consider a route 1.5 times slower than the fastest (PDF p.1, p.16). The band differs widely from person to person: for about half of participants it lies between 15% and 50% (PDF p.13).
- The label counts: among routes close in time, the one presented as fastest is still preferred, 'even for small relative differences'; 'just the fact of informing a route to be the fastest increases its probability to be chosen', by about 10 percentage points over the second fastest (PDF p.12, p.16).
- What decides among routes that are close in time: 'participants put more stress on the physical route attributes rather than on travel time information'; directness is the most important, and fewer intersections are preferred (PDF p.15-16).
- Two kinds of traveller suggested by the model: 'one taking decisions mainly based on the travel time, and the other based on the directness or length of the trip' (PDF p.15).
- Field studies the paper cites, from GPS traces and number plates: the share of real trips that follow the fastest route ranges from 25% (Reggio Emilia, 89 travellers, routes on average 1.3 times longer than the shortest) to 75% (Enschede); in Minneapolis-St. Paul 40% of trips followed the fastest path and almost 90% a route no more than 5 minutes longer (PDF p.1-2).
- The authors' inference: since their participants had the times in front of them, in real conditions 'the choices for the fastest route cannot be more than 60.5%' (PDF p.16).
- No learning was observed across repeated choices, by design: choices were made several at a time and each pair came up about twice (PDF p.4-5).
- About 2% to 5% of participants accepted routes more than twice as slow as the fastest; the authors think they 'were not engaged in the experiments' (PDF p.13).

## Limits

A game on a screen with nothing at stake: no real trip, no cost, no lateness, and travel times presented as certain. Two thirds of the participants are students of traffic theory. Car routes inside one city, trips of about 4 to 40 minutes; nothing on public transport, on changes or on a journey in several legs. Only three routes per choice. It measures the choice of a route for a trip the person has never made, not the habitual choice of a commuter. The 31% band is an average of very different people, and its definition (the 95th percentile of each person's choices) is the authors' choice among three they tried.
