## What it says

A study of 5,326 Dutch adults with a three-day travel diary (Netherlands Mobility Panel, 2018). People like the car most, whatever they actually use. People who mix several modes, above all those who use bus/tram/metro or train, are far more likely to use a mode they have a negative attitude to. So mixing modes often goes with using something disliked, not with liking it, and without support such people may move toward the car.

## Data and quotes used

- Method: Netherlands Mobility Panel 2018; 5,326 people aged 18+ from 3,314 households; three-day location-based diary; attitudes on seven outcomes (comfort, relaxation, pleasure, flexibility, time saving, safety, prestige), each weighted by how important the person finds it; five clusters by k-means. Sample over-represents car owners (76%) (PDF p.3-4).
- 68% of people used more than one mode in three days; but only 5.5% of trips used more than two modes, 1.8% more than three, 0.6% more than four. Average 9.0 trips and 10.4 trip segments per person in three days (PDF p.4-5).
- Share of trip segments: car 0.49, bicycle 0.24, walking 0.21, train 0.02, bus/tram/metro 0.02 (table 1, PDF p.5).
- Attitude scores (sum, range -56 to +56): car 20.96, bicycle 14.58, walking 12.22, train 4.06, bus/tram/metro -1.97. Share with a positive attitude: car 94%, cycling 85%, walking 83%, train 59%, bus/tram/metro 37%. Bus/tram/metro scores lowest on pleasure, relaxation and comfort (PDF p.5).
- Clusters: car mostly 43.9% (car = 86% of segments); bicycle mostly 23.7%; walk + car + bicycle 18.8%; multimodal with bus/tram/metro 6.3%; multimodal with train 7.6% (train = 19% of segments, walking 34%, bicycle 25%, car 16%; 17.0 segments in three days against 8.4 for car users; 85.1% hold a public transport subscription; 25.8% students; 46.2% own a car) (table 3, PDF p.6-7).
- Even in the multimodal train cluster the car is liked more than the train: car 18.27, train 10.18 (table 4, PDF p.7). 'Attitudes towards car use are most positive, independent of the cluster membership and levels of multimodality' (PDF p.1).
- People with a negative attitude to a mode they use: 3.6% for their most used mode, 8.7% for the first or second most used, 12.6% for any mode they used (PDF p.5). Against car-mostly users, the odds of using a disliked mode are 5.8 to 10.5 times higher for the two multimodal public transport clusters (table 5, PDF p.8).
- Which mode is disliked: among incompatible people in the multimodal train cluster, 47.7% dislike the train, 41.2% bus/tram/metro, 28.8% walking (mode set); for the most used mode, 63-73% of incompatible multimodal public transport users dislike walking (appendix B, PDF p.12-13). The authors: walking is required as access and egress to public transport, while car users can simply walk less (PDF p.10).
- Quote: 'multimodal individuals in general use modes they dislike' (PDF p.10). Proposed reasons: cost, no car (students with free or subsidised public transport), i.e. control over the choice matters more than attitude (PDF p.9-10).
- Warning for policy: 'voluntary modal changes for multimodal individuals may not occur towards a more sustainable direction; in the worst-case scenario, the dominant role of cars in daily travelling could increase' (PDF p.11). Cited: car-dominant users overestimate public transport time and cost, and their satisfaction rises after one month of use (PDF p.10).

## Limits

Netherlands: cycling country with dense rail; the authors say results may not transfer. 'Multimodal' here means using several modes over three days, mostly on separate trips, not combining them door to door in one trip. Attitude is measured on seven items only (no cost, environment, health, privacy). The paper does not measure discomfort, so 'dissonance' is inferred. Not specific to commuting or to long distances. Data from 2018, before the pandemic.
