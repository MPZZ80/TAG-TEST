## What it says

A survey of 288 people who commute by high-speed train from Suzhou to Shanghai (about 30 minutes on the train). It is the only source in this research that studies intercity rail commuters door to door. More commuters say their health got worse than better. What goes with better health is having commuted the same distance by slower means before, walking or cycling to and from the stations, a short trip from home to the station, and living near family and friends. Getting from the arrival station to the workplace takes over 30 minutes for 72% of them, more than the train itself. The authors ask for a door-to-door view and note that operators do not design high-speed services for commuters.

## Data and quotes used

- Method: questionnaire in July 2018 at four high-speed stations in Suzhou during the morning peak (7-10) and through two messaging groups of commuters; filled in on the phone during the train ride; 288 valid answers; purposive sample, because the number of such commuters is unknown; ordinal logistic regression. Self-reported change in health 'after commuting by high-speed railway' on five levels (PDF p.4-8).
- Who they are: 80% men; average age 34.6; 98% with a degree; 40% managers, 25% technicians, 16% professionals; 44% earn over 20,000 RMB a month; 75% own a car; 78% live with family; 54% married with one child; 53% work 40-50 hours a week (table 1, PDF p.6-7, p.10).
- 74% became long-distance commuters after high-speed rail arrived; 26% already commuted between the two cities by other means (PDF p.8). The train makes people who would have moved or not taken the job into commuters: 'a substitute for migration' (PDF p.12).
- Health: physical health worse for 38%, unchanged 32%, better 30%; mental health worse 36%, unchanged 36%, better 28%. Among new long-distance commuters 46% report worse physical health and 22% better; among those who commuted before, more than half report better (PDF p.8).
- First leg, home to station (Suzhou): by car 55%, public transport 36%, on foot 5%, bicycle 5%; under 30 minutes for 69%, 30-60 minutes 27%, over 60 minutes 4% (table 1, PDF p.6).
- Last leg, station to workplace (Shanghai): public transport 77%, car 12%, on foot 9%, bicycle 2%; under 30 minutes for only 28%, 30-60 minutes 49%, over 60 minutes 23% (table 1, PDF p.6). With about 30 minutes on the train, the two urban legs are most of the door-to-door time.
- What goes with better health in the model: having been a long-distance commuter before (new commuters have odds of 0.20 for physical and 0.25 for mental health); walking from home to the station (odds 4.63 against car); a transfer from home under 30 minutes (odds 8.48 against over 60) or 30-60 minutes (6.17); cycling from the station to work (16.81, on very few people); agreeing strongly that the commute keeps them close to friends and relatives (4.07) (table 2, PDF p.9, p.11).
- What goes with worse health: a low rent or mortgage, read as poorer housing (odds 0.28); working as a technician or professional (0.33 and 0.31 for mental health) (PDF p.12).
- Why people do it: 76% agree it gives a better-paid job, 80% career opportunities; only 47% a better quality of life and 52% a more affordable cost of living (PDF p.8).
- Cost: 87% spend less than 15% of monthly income on the train; 40% receive a travel allowance from the company; no season ticket exists for these trains (PDF p.10, p.12).
- The authors' caveat on speed: 'HSR's potential time-saving does not necessarily result in reduced commuting time when the door-to-door journey (both the first and the last mile) is taken into account'; stations outside dense areas have poor access (PDF p.2, p.13).
- On operators: 'most national and regional rail operators welcome business trips and discourage commuting. As a result, HSR commuting is largely a users' aspiration and struggle rather than the intention of HSR operators' (PDF p.13).
- Literature cited: long commutes, above all over 60 minutes, go with stress, poor sleep and less time for healthy activities; in France many users of high-speed trains from cities within an hour of Paris hold season tickets (PDF p.2-3).
- What the study did not ask, in the authors' words: comfort on board, convenience of the transfers, whether ticketing is user-friendly or offers season tickets, whether timetables are integrated and frequent enough (PDF p.12).

## Limits

China, one pair of cities with a very large city at one end; context outside the scope, used for the mechanism only. A convenience sample caught at stations and in commuter chat groups: 80% men and high earners. Health is one self-rated question asked once, with people comparing before and after from memory. Some odds rest on very few people (2% cycle from the station). Cross-section: no causal claim. Nothing on information, tools, disruptions or tickets.
