## What it says

A statistical model of which means of transport 1,615 urban commuters in England use for the longest part of their trip to work, built on a government survey of 2009-2010. Being a habitual driver and having vehicles in the household cut the odds of every alternative to the car by about nine tenths. High income goes with the train, not the car. Attitudes towards the bus predict use better than the distance to the stop or the frequency of the service, which drop out of the model. Train commuters are the long-distance ones.

## Data and quotes used

- Method: secondary analysis of a Department for Transport survey of nearly 4,000 people in England interviewed in 2009 and 2010 to build a segmentation of attitudes; subset of 1,615 urban commuters; multinomial logistic regression with the car as the reference; the question was which mode is used 'for the longest part of the journey'; the survey was taken during a recession and the sample is a little older than urban commuters in the census (PDF p.4, p.9).
- Mode of the 1,615 urban commuters: car, van or motorbike 63%; walking or cycling 16%; bus 12%; train, underground or tram 9% (table 1, PDF p.4). In the 2011 census: car 65%, active 15%, train 11%, bus 9% (PDF p.4).
- Habit: a 'habitual driver' is someone who uses a car at least weekly and agrees that travelling by car is something done frequently, 'typically me' and 'automatically'. Being one multiplies the odds of commuting by active modes by 0.14, by bus by 0.07 and by train by 0.10, with everything else held constant (table 4, PDF p.5, p.7).
- Owning is not the same as using by habit: 83% of these commuters had one or more vehicles in the household 'while just over half were habitual drivers', and both variables keep their own effect in the model (PDF p.8).
- Each additional vehicle in the household: odds 0.33 for active modes, 0.07 for bus, 0.10 for train (table 4, PDF p.7).
- Distance to work: each step up in distance raises the odds of the train (1.15) and lowers those of walking and cycling (0.59) (table 4, PDF p.7).
- Income: a household income of 60,000 pounds or more multiplies the odds of commuting by train by 15.13 (confidence interval 2.99 to 76.56) and by active modes by 4.76; an income of 12,500 to 19,999 pounds multiplies the odds of the bus by 2.87 (table 4, PDF p.7).
- Living in London multiplies the odds of the train by 6.95 (confidence interval 1.63 to 29.67) (table 4, PDF p.7).
- Age: people in their twenties and in their fifties have much lower odds of commuting by train (0.06 and 0.02), which the authors think 'could be linked to ticket cost' (table 4, PDF p.7).
- Attitudes beat service levels: walking time to the bus stop, bus frequency and walking time to the station all had an effect alone and lost it in the full model, while attitudes kept theirs. Definitely disagreeing with 'I would only travel by bus if I had no other choice' multiplies the odds of the bus by 7.59 (PDF p.6-8, table 4).
- The authors' reading: 'personal views about buses have more influence on commuting mode than proximity to bus stops'; and the caveat, since the survey is a single snapshot: it 'could equally imply that bus commuters have positive attitudes towards buses, whereas those who do not regularly use buses perceive them more negatively' (PDF p.8).
- Access to the station when tested alone: a station more than 27 minutes' walk away lowers the odds of the train, and one more than 43 minutes away lowers the odds of the bus as well; not knowing how often buses leave the nearest stop goes with not using the bus (table 2, PDF p.6).
- Statements about the train that had at least one significant result when tested by group: 'I would only travel by train if I had no other choice' (3 results), 'I find travelling by train is expensive' (2), 'I find travelling by train stressful' (1), 'I like travelling by train' (1); 'I enjoy driving' had none and 'I find driving stressful' had 3 (table 3, PDF p.7).
- Not in the survey, by the authors' own account: 'Urban congestion, parking availability, and costs are not captured' (PDF p.9).

## Limits

Data from 2009 and 2010, well before the period this research prefers and before hybrid work. Urban commuters in England only; rural commuters were left out for lack of cases, and intercity commuting is not identified. Only the mode of the longest part of the trip is recorded, so the legs to and from the station do not appear. Train commuters are 137 people, and the confidence intervals on the train results are very wide. A single cross-section: the direction between attitude and use cannot be told, as the authors say. The simplest form of choice model. Nothing on information, planning, tickets or disruption.
