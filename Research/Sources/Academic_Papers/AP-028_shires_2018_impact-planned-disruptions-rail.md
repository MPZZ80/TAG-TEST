## What it says

A British study of how rail passengers react to planned engineering works (not commuters): about 7,000 passengers surveyed on trains and 456 in an online panel. A replacement bus loses about three times more rail passengers than a diverted train. Knowing about the disruption before reaching the station changes behaviour little. Passengers treat a planned disruption as a fixed cost more than counting the extra minutes; a guaranteed connection is worth about 9 minutes, and a disruption that lasts a week instead of a day costs about 22 minutes more. Over the following year about 10-13% of rail trips were lost.

## Data and quotes used

- Method: about 12,000 forms handed out on trains in Great Britain, over 9,000 returned, nearly 7,000 used; online panel of 500 (456 used). Two kinds of data: what people did the last time (revealed) and what they say they would do (stated). Commuters were excluded on purpose: routine works are mostly at weekends, and season ticket holders are reimbursed (PDF p.3-4, 12).
- Scale of the problem: on an average weekday in October 2017 there were 39 engineering disruptions (62% served by replacement bus), 71 on a Saturday, 92 on a Sunday (table 1, PDF p.5). Cancellations and significant lateness were 3.8% of English operators' services in 2016/17; compensation to operators for planned works was 316 million pounds in 2015-16 (PDF p.6).
- Example of the time cost: London-Cardiff normally about 2 h; diverted train about 2 h 45; replacement bus 3 h to 3 h 45 (PDF p.5). A replacement bus adds a fixed penalty for the change, connection time is usually weighted twice in-vehicle time, and bus time weighs more than train time (PDF p.5, footnote 6).
- What people actually did when they knew in advance: 66-74% took the replacement bus and on average 19.5% left rail (other mode or no trip); with a diverted train 67-69% stayed and about 9% left rail. Stated answers exaggerate: 32% and 21% said they would leave (PDF p.15).
- Quote: 'three times more rail demand is lost with bus replacement than with rail diversion' (PDF p.28).
- Awareness: 'The level of awareness prior to arriving at the station does not seem to have a large impact on the pattern of behavioural response'; on average awareness raises the use of another station by about 3 points, another route by about 2, and not using rail by about 5. The authors link this to information 'on the go via mobile devices' (PDF p.15, 28). An earlier industry study found 53% aware and 47% unaware (PDF p.7).
- Fixed cost: 'rail travellers see planned disruptions as a fixed cost and are not particularly sensitive to the amount of additional time when making their decisions' (PDF p.28).
- Guaranteed connection is worth about 9 minutes on a long-distance London trip (about 2.5 times more for airport trips); a disruption lasting a week instead of a day costs about 22 minutes more: travellers 'are prepared to tolerate a one-off disruption but are much more averse to it being an ongoing event' (PDF p.18, 29).
- Business travellers are 16% more sensitive to extra time; Sunday travellers 7% less (PDF p.18).
- Longer term (online panel, past 12 months): no difference 64%; less likely to use rail for the disrupted journey 18%; less likely for any journey 7%; no longer use rail for the disrupted journey 4%; no longer for any journey 6% (table 12, PDF p.26). Overall loss of rail trips 10-13% (PDF p.27).
- Mechanism proposed: 'when faced with disruption, passengers identify alternative behaviours which then manifests as a moderate increasing reluctance to make rail journeys in the long term' (PDF p.27). People who try another mode during a disruption may keep it (PDF p.3).
- General frame for any disrupted trip: continue; change destination or activity; keep the same mode and route; reschedule; cancel (PDF p.3).
- The authors note that an unplanned disruption that goes on has the same features as a planned one once travellers know about it (PDF p.3).

## Limits

Commuters are excluded by design, so the figures describe occasional and leisure or business travellers, not the project's users; the authors expect commuters to be less able to leave rail. Great Britain, data collected about 2016. Funded by the association of train operators. Planned works only, not strikes or sudden delays. Several tables did not convert, so the model coefficients and the full response table were not read. The longer-term loss rests on what people remember and on assumed trip rates; the authors call it an upper bound for one disruption.
