## What it says

The European Commission's impact assessment for two proposed regulations of May 2026, one on multimodal booking platforms and one on rail ticketing. It documents with a representative survey and a route analysis that travellers cannot see, compare and buy all options in one place, especially rail trips with more than one operator, and explains it by the market position of incumbent rail companies that own the main ticket platforms. It concerns regional and long-distance ticket sales; local and urban transport is outside its scope.

## Data and quotes used

- Problem 1: lack of transparency, unfair conditions and uneven playing field in the online ticketing market. Problem 2: incomplete offer of rail tickets on rail platforms (PDF p.11).
- The proposals do not apply to local or urban transport services (PDF p.6, note 3): the first and last legs of a door-to-door commute stay outside the single ticket.
- Flash Eurobarometer 551 (summer 2024, 25,805 online interviews, representative of the EU aged 15 and over): 31% of respondents had never booked a journey combining modes and 36% never one combining operators in the same mode; over a third of those who had found it difficult (PDF p.13, p.96-97).
- Reasons given for never or rarely booking combined journeys: 17% could not find a suitable combination, 5% lacked information on where to search, 5% could not buy all tickets in one place; for rail journeys with several operators the figures are higher: 23%, 11% and 9% (PDF p.13, p.19).
- 76% of respondents book transport tickets online; ease of finding travel options and of booking are among the most important factors when booking (PDF p.97).
- 78% say the environmental impact of travel matters to them, but 21% adapt their journeys; 22% find it hard to change habits and 22% find it hard to find sustainable alternatives (PDF p.12, p.97).
- On 100 origin-destination routes in the EU, multimodal options existed on 76%, but 39% of possible rail or coach connections were not visible on the comparison platform used as benchmark, and rail options were entirely missing in 29% of cases; platforms that show several modes rarely combine them (PDF p.12).
- The Commission's own estimate of what better ticketing could move is small: the maximum potential increase in rail journeys is put at 1.7%, the share who knew what to buy and could not buy it in one place (PDF p.13).
- Journey planners that give information but do not sell or link to tickets, such as Google Maps and Citymapper, are not counted as multimodal digital mobility services and are outside the rules (PDF p.14).
- Incumbent rail companies' own channels hold most online sales: about 90% of visits for long-distance rail tickets in Germany go to bahn.de; SNCF Connect is estimated at 80-90% in France; Renfe's own channels at 80-90% in Spain (PDF p.18 and note 69).
- A court in Frankfurt in October 2023 forbade the DB Navigator app from presetting a 'fastest connection' filter that favoured DB's own trains over faster alternatives (PDF p.16).
- On the ÖBB website the competitor Westbahn appears only in the trip planner, without price and without the possibility to buy (PDF p.19).
- A YouGov poll for Transport & Environment (10,514 adults in seven countries, Italy included): 61% of long-distance rail users met difficulties when booking, 45.6% want to combine tickets of different operators on one website, 43% say they would travel more by rail if booking were easier (PDF p.21).
- A 2021 study by St. Pölten University: about a third of 76 participants failed to buy an international train ticket; rail bookings took 15-30 minutes against 5-10 for flights (PDF p.20).
- Two tickets for one journey mean no passenger rights if a connection is missed; the parallel revision of rail passenger rights would treat tickets bought in one transaction on one platform as a single ticket (PDF p.6, p.9, p.12).
- National access points must hold static, historic, observed and dynamic data of the different modes, but platforms that sell tickets need further data such as real-time fares (PDF p.8).
- The European passengers' federation says that access to data, including real-time information on disruptions, is what enables such services (PDF p.98).
- Second reading. What the Commission says travellers do today: 'In the baseline scenario, travellers must dedicate time and effort to search for the most convenient offer in terms of fares, comfort and travel time, with no guarantee that they will manage to find the offer that best matches their preferences' (PDF p.51). The saving in search effort is named as the most direct benefit for users, and it is the one benefit the document could not put a number on: 'difficult to quantify and thus only assessed qualitatively' (PDF p.53, p.68, p.109)
- Habit and trust in the Commission's own words: in most Member States the incumbent railway's platform 'is still the go-to platform for passengers'; where it sells some other companies but not all 'it is difficult for passengers to see that they are being presented with an incomplete offer'; 'Even if the use of AI tools would help passengers in revealing more convenient offers, habits are not easily changed and confidence in alternative purchasing channels is not easily established' (PDF p.66). 'The inertia in consumer behaviour might however dampen the effect as many consumers would remain loyal' to those platforms (PDF p.52)
- Flash Eurobarometer 551, detail by answer (Annex 4, Table 42, PDF p.127-128; shares of those asked, without 'don't know'). Why people never or rarely combine modes or operators: 29% 'It was not needed (one transport service/operator brought me to my destination)', 22% 'The journey would have taken too long', 17% more expensive, 14% 'I could not find a suitable combination', 12% 'I did not want to buy several separate tickets and risk being stranded if I miss a connection', 6% did not know it was possible, 4% did not know where to search, 4% 'I could not buy all the tickets I needed in one place'. For rail with several operators: 42% not needed, 6% could not buy all tickets in one place. Barriers to booking online: 36% 'None of these', 19% hidden costs, 15% customer support hard to reach online, 14% completeness of the information, 11% 'Websites or apps to book journeys are difficult to use'
- Same survey: 76% book tickets online but 'only a minority of them (26%) do so after comparing travel options and prices'; when planning a regional or long-distance journey cost matters most (61%) and duration next (47%), ease of finding travel options 17% and ease of booking 16% (PDF p.131). As a share of all respondents: 5.7% could not find a suitable combination, 2.3% did not know it was possible, 1.8% did not know where to search, 1.7% could not buy all tickets in one place, 8.8% at least one of these (PDF p.132). Young people aged 15-24 search for combined trips and find them more often, and more often give up because they could not book everything in one place (PDF p.61)
- How small the expected effect is: the ceiling of 1.7% more journeys by rail and coach is taken from the 1.7% above; the preferred option reaches 1.19% from 2035 (PDF p.132, p.153). Passenger-km against the baseline with the preferred option: car -0.4% in 2030 and -1.1% in 2050, rail +2.5% and +4.8%, coach +1.3% and +2.8% (Table 16, PDF p.57). Spending per person on interurban travel: -0.34% by 2050 (Table 14, PDF p.53). Without the savings to society from the modal shift every option costs more than it returns (benefit to cost 0.9 for the preferred option); those savings are about 99% of the benefits (PDF p.67-68)
- The Commission's scrutiny board: negative opinion on 13 September 2023, then positive 'with reservations' on 13 April 2026. The second opinion still says 'The existence and magnitude of the alleged market failure(s) ... are not sufficiently demonstrated' (PDF p.87) and that the modal shift rests on 'stated preferences, expert judgment and opinions of stakeholders. Consequently, the benefits resulting from the estimated modal shift should not be included in the cost benefit analysis' (PDF p.88-89). Experts and stakeholders 'indicated the difficulty to derive a modal shift potential ... since there have been little relevant applications of similar initiatives' (PDF p.42, p.131). At the workshop of 12 June 2025 (108 attendees) 36% found the 1.7% too high, 41% too low, 23% about right (PDF p.133)
- What is outside the proposal. First and last leg: measures to make platforms 'include active modes of transport (walking and cycling) for the first/last leg of a trip' were discarded as 'not in scope of the initiative, which focuses on platforms selling regional and long-distance transport services' (Annex 7, PDF p.227). 'The urban dimension is out of scope of the analysis' (PDF p.85). Platforms that only give information and platforms 'operating only within cities' are not counted (PDF p.161-162). The route analysis 'assumed travel from city centre to city centre', counted 'time in transit only, and only for major connections' and left out connections under 10 minutes on foot, tram or metro (PDF p.134). Rules for continuing a journey after a missed connection were moved to the rail passenger rights revision (PDF p.81, p.228)
- The 100-route test includes pairs of commuting length, for example Busto Arsizio-Milan 35.3 km, Hoorn-Amsterdam 40.2 km, Dublin-Drogheda 52.2 km, Ingolstadt-Munich 80.3 km, Barcelona-Girona 94.6 km (Table 38, PDF p.118-119). The platform side was tested on one product, Omio, which offers 'within-mode travel' only, 'multimodal connections not being included'; the reference was Google Maps plus the operators' sites (PDF p.133). Trains missing on the platform were 'slightly more concentrated in domestic routes (17 of 29 instances)'; booking direct gave a faster connection on 17% of land and sea routes; the price comparison used different dates and is 'likely ... overestimated' (PDF p.121)
- The market counted: 364 platforms that sell or link to tickets in the EU in 2025, 266 for consumers and 98 for businesses; 'There is no European database of these services'. 'Although 364 MDMS platforms have been identified, 14 of the 27 EU Member States have at most one platform offering more than basic travel information for land-based modes. No platform currently provides EU-wide direct booking across air, land, and water modes; even the most advanced only offer re-linking functionality' (PDF p.162, p.165). Italy is classed with France, Poland and Belgium as a 'planned adopter', not a leader; leaders are Germany, Austria, Denmark, Luxembourg, Finland, Spain and the Netherlands (Table 40, PDF p.123)
- Shares: independent platforms sold about 3% of EU rail tickets in 2019 (about 65 million), projected at 4.1% in 2030; coach 2.5%; all modes 6.7% (PDF p.33, p.167-169). Around 49% of air tickets in the EU went through intermediaries in 2024 (PDF p.237). Combined trips counted in the baseline are almost all with a flight: 91 million multimodal passengers in 2019 out of 12.9 billion, over 60% air-rail, over 30% air-coach, and about 1 million rail-coach (Table 50, PDF p.160-161); the count leaves out local and suburban transport and the car
- Why integration is slow, supply side: operators counted are 3,778 bus and coach companies running services above 50 km, 3,211 of them small or medium firms, 158 railway undertakings, 523 waterborne and 331 air carriers (Table 57, PDF p.165-166). A national or regional platform has agreements with 8 to 100 operators (median 20), a Europe-wide one with 16 to 500 (Table 58, PDF p.166). One agreement costs about EUR 20,000 to negotiate according to a rail operator, and 'a public transport operator suggested 6 months to a year of negotiation time'; agreements are renegotiated about every three years (PDF p.167, p.200). 'Voluntary approaches have failed to deliver seamless multimodal access: vertically integrated platforms see themselves primarily as rail operators' (PDF p.71). A platform reports that 'some operators restrict the information that can be shown on MDMS platforms or impose prohibitively low commission rates ... redirecting users to operator websites for modifications, which disrupts the user experience' (PDF p.147); 'some smaller regional RUs have no desire for their tickets to be available on other platforms' (PDF p.149); in Germany and France 'regional authorities - not operators - control fare and ticketing policies' (PDF p.144)
- A limit stated by operators and consumer bodies: 'On low-demand routes ... even full information availability is unlikely to trigger substantial behavioural change, as barriers such as frequency, speed, and service coverage dominate user preferences (interview with CER and BEUC)' (PDF p.144)
- Competition cases on rail ticket selling (Annex 11, PDF p.246-248). Italy, 18 April 2023: the competition authority accepted commitments from Trenitalia so that the competitor NTV can sell Trenitalia regional and intercity public-service tickets, and regional connections of Trenitalia TPER and Trenord, together with its own. Germany, 2023: Deutsche Bahn was found to withhold real-time data from third-party platforms; from 2024 platforms receive 'real-time data on delays and cancellations and up-to-date information on departure platforms and major disruptions'; an appeal was still open in March 2026. Spain, 2024: Renfe committed to give all content and real-time data to third-party platforms. Sweden, 2019: a neutral booking platform exists 'but sells a very small number of tickets'
- Stakeholders are split: the obligations to share tickets and to host competitors are supported by most national authorities, moderately by platforms and 'mostly opposed by transport operators' (PDF p.76). Read and not relevant to the commute: costs of each measure by Member State, the modelling tools, air ticket distribution and the reservation systems code (PDF p.43-50, 113-117, 154-159, 170-217, 237-245)

## Limits

A document written to justify a legislative proposal: it selects evidence that supports intervention. Its first version received a negative opinion from the Commission's scrutiny board and the revised one a positive opinion with reservations, which still found the market failure not sufficiently demonstrated. It is about buying tickets for regional, long-distance and cross-border trips, mostly occasional ones; it says nothing on commuters, season tickets or daily repeated trips, and excludes urban transport, the car legs and the first and last leg on foot or by bike. The effects on travel are modelled from stated preferences in one survey and from expert scoring, not observed. The route test used one platform (Omio) on 100 routes chosen by the consultants. The Eurobarometer shares differ between the main text (PDF p.12, p.97) and the annex table (PDF p.127-128) because the bases differ; quote each with its page. Stakeholder surveys are small and not representative (about 70 respondents); the expert workshop had at most 11 votes. Much of the document concerns air ticket distribution, not relevant here.
