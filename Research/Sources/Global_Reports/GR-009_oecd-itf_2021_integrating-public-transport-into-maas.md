## What it says

The summary of an OECD International Transport Forum roundtable held in October 2020 on Mobility as a Service: what it is, how it can be organised, what it offers users, how it should be regulated, how tickets can be resold and how data should be shared. Its main message for this project is a cautious one: the demand for MaaS and its business case are not proven, pilots were too short and small to generalise, users choose by convenience and not by sustainability, and habitual public transport users tend to keep one mode and the operator's own app.

## Data and quotes used

- MaaS is defined as a model for supplying a wide range of passenger transport services through a single digital customer interface, integrating transport, information and payment services (PDF p.6).
- "Most visions of MaaS assume that it will increase the demand for public transport, but this impact remains to be demonstrated at scale"; "the business case for MaaS is not yet proven" (PDF p.6, p.34).
- One participant reported that even where public transport tickets were available through MaaS in their region, ticket sales had not been high (PDF p.13).
- For people who already use public transport, "day-to-day trips tend to be routine, and users have established habits, routes and destinations. Many operators also already have their own apps and passes"; even with many options a user may keep a subset of modes for routine trips (PDF p.14).
- A 2015 EU study found that 78% of respondents use only one mode for their most frequent trip (Fiorello and Zani 2015, cited at PDF p.14).
- Some participants argued that "international and intercity seamlessness is of little importance among the user base" and more relevant to a niche, high-income audience than to routine travel (PDF p.14).
- "MaaS must be a better offer than currently available options"; a first and last mile MaaS built to feed a trunk public transport line will not work if that line is unattractive or the trip is less convenient than by car (PDF p.14).
- In the pilots reviewed, the environment was never the top reason for trying MaaS; convenience and novelty ranked highest. In a Dutch survey environmental sustainability was considered by about 40% of respondents and under 25% among heavy car users (PDF p.14).
- Pilots did not point to one identifiable target audience, and "build it and they will come" is unlikely to work for a MaaS app; frequent public transport users are among the more promising groups (PDF p.14).
- Pilots were mostly too short or too small to generalise; users need time to learn a system, and people are not eager to join transport pilots they expect to end, because long-term reliability of a transport offer matters to their lives. A pilot should last at least six months, preferably a year (PDF p.16-17).
- The private car has a low marginal cost at the point of use because its fixed costs are already paid, which makes alternatives look expensive; participants did not expect a significant shift from cars without raising the cost of car travel (PDF p.13, p.21).
- Investment in interchange facilities is named as a way "to reduce the stress of multimodal connections" (PDF p.21).
- Data flows are what give MaaS its advantages: intermodal ticketing, real-time passenger information, routing and scheduling across operators. Operators fear losing the customer relationship and that the MaaS provider becomes the 'gatekeeper' who decides which options the trip planner shows (PDF p.27-28).
- A business-to-business market exists: in Belgium employers give a mobility budget in place of a company car, managed through payment cards (PDF p.15, p.22).
- In the United States some transit agencies offer a 'guaranteed ride home': a subsidised ride-hailing trip that guarantees commuters a way back (PDF p.19).
- Women's travel is more complex, with more trips chained together, and costs more when several tickets are needed (PDF p.13).

## Limits

A summary of expert discussion, not a study with its own data; figures are quoted from other works that were not opened. Written in 2020-2021, at the start of the pandemic, so the market it describes has changed (several MaaS companies it names have since closed or been sold: this is my knowledge, not in the source). The focus is urban; intercity travel is mentioned once and dismissed as a niche, which is an opinion of some participants, not evidence.
