## What it says

Recommendations of the European Commission's expert group on urban mobility, adopted in June 2024, on how shared and on-demand mobility should complement public transport in cities, suburbs and rural areas. It is a policy text: challenges, recommendations to authorities and thirteen short case studies supplied by operators and cities. The useful parts for this project are what it says about the first and last mile, about hubs at stations, and the few figures in the case studies.

## Data and quotes used

- The document states that transport poverty is a result of transport injustice and that in suburban, peri-urban and rural areas those who can barely afford a car spend a disproportionate amount on it (PDF p.7, section The Challenge).
- The group states the door-to-door aim directly: 'The complementarity of the different modes of transport provides travellers with the best, safe, reliable and accessible mobility options from door to door', and says further effort is needed 'especially to cover the first and last mile trips' (PDF p.8, p.24).
- It describes who is stuck: in suburban, peri-urban and rural areas 'those who can (even if barely) afford a car must spend a disproportionate amount of the family's revenue on it, and those who can't, must spend a disproportionate amount of their time on a public transportation that has inherent limitations in those areas' (PDF p.7).
- 'The real challenge is not shifting from one mode to another, but rather shifting from a one-mode monopoly of individual cars to a multimodal freedom of choice' (PDF p.8).
- For daily commutes, bringing a bike or scooter on board trains or intercity buses 'can meet certain needs when there is no alternative to reach the final destination' or as a backup in case of technical issues or bad weather, but it is 'not the primary lever'; the priority is bike racks and secure lockers at all public transport stops (PDF p.19).
- One of the biggest challenges for MaaS is sharing operational data 'safely and securely, accurately and on-time'; without a governance framework 'private interests do not naturally align with sustainable mobility goals' (PDF p.14, p.21).
- Geneva, 2023: 48% of trips on the shared bikes integrated with public transport replaced public transport journeys at rush hour and 13% replaced car journeys; the figures come from the bike operator (PDF p.28-29).
- Denmark, 2022: the national railway and a bike-sharing company placed shared bikes at stations to connect commuters to workplaces in a suburb of Copenhagen; a separate app was dropped in favour of the bike operator's existing app, and employers pay for the service (PDF p.27-28).
- Lyon, 2021: holders of a public transport pass given free unlocking of shared e-scooters took 30% more rides, 25% shorter, read as more first and last mile use; in a survey in four cities 86% said they combined public transport and scooters more. Figures from the scooter operator (PDF p.31-32).
- Operators' data: first and last mile trips that complement public transport are 41% to 55% of shared micromobility trips in 2021-2022 (PDF p.30).
- Berlin: a ride-hailing last-mile service at a fixed price of 6 euros from more than 220 metro and suburban rail stations dispatches over 50,000 rides a year, most of them at night (PDF p.32-33).
- Spain: the national railway's MaaS platform dōcō aims to provide door-to-door services by combining trains, taxis and ride-hailing, and mopeds (PDF p.33).
- Employers should be enabled to give incentives to employees who use shared mobility for home-work journeys, through a 'sustainable mobility package' (PDF p.17).

## Limits

A consensus policy document by an expert group that includes industry associations; recommendations, not research. The case study figures are supplied by the operators that benefit from them, without method or sample. The focus is urban and peri-urban, not intercity; commuting is mentioned but not studied.
