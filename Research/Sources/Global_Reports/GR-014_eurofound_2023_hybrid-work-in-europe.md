## What it says

A Eurofound report of 2023 on what 'hybrid work' means and how it was put in place in the European Union during the pandemic, built from a literature review and reports by national correspondents collected between December 2021 and January 2022. It is about work organisation, not transport. For this project it gives the share of jobs that can be done remotely and shows that reduced commuting is named as a benefit of hybrid work for workers, employers and society, without measuring it.

## Data and quotes used

- Companies show a tendency to occupy office buildings in more central areas, close to employee housing, because this saves commuting time (PDF p.25).
- 37% of dependent employment in the EU can be done by telework, between 33% and 44% across Member States, with large differences between high and low earners and between white- and blue-collar workers (Sostero et al. 2020, cited at PDF p.8).
- Reduced commuting appears among the reasons why organisations adopted hybrid work, next to lower office costs and attracting staff (PDF p.29).
- 'Time and cost savings from reduced commuting' are listed among the benefits expected by individual workers; at the level of society the benefits named are fewer traffic jams, lower emissions and time saved (PDF p.36).
- A study commissioned by Greenpeace Germany in 2020 estimated that if 40% of employees worked from home two days a week, emissions from commuter traffic would fall by 18%; Eurofound's own study found the effect of telework on emissions complex and very hard to calculate (PDF p.29).
- The literature review cites a typology of mobile workers: 'pendulums' have two recurrent work locations, 'yo-yos' return to a main workplace, 'nomads' work in more than two places; the more locations and the greater the distance between them, the greater the need to coordinate (Lilischkis 2003, cited at PDF p.11).
- Vehicles such as cars, buses, trams and trains are listed among the possible workplaces in hybrid work (PDF p.12).
- Second pass. Method of the examples of practice: 80 examples of hybrid work in organisations in the 27 Member States, collected by national correspondents at the end of 2021, when 'most companies did not yet have much experience with hybrid work'; 23 in finance, 19 in ICT and telecommunications (PDF p.27).
- What the agreements fix: of 107 policy elements found, 40 set 'a specific number of days/weeks in the office', 15 leave the employee free to choose the location, 7 set a share of monthly working time, 5 leave it to the team. 'Most organisations required employees to spend one to three days in the office per week' (table 6, PDF p.27; p.28).
- Who decides the days is named as the critical issue: 'One critical issue in hybrid work is who – the employer or the employee – is authorised to decide how much time is spent teleworking and how much is spent in the office' (PDF p.13).
- Timing as part of the definition: hybrid working 'may entail working at the employer's premises only on Mondays and Fridays', or at home in the morning and at the office in the afternoon; frequency ranges from once a month to every week (PDF p.13).
- Commuting in the motives: of 27 stated reasons for introducing hybrid work, 2 are 'to eliminate the commute'; productivity, motivation and well-being lead with 8 (table 6, PDF p.28). At the level of society, 'reduced commuting' is named 4 times among the expected benefits (table 7, PDF p.35).
- Places between home and the office: 'Some organisations reported establishing working hubs in more remote areas for those employees who lived further away from the main office'; some companies moved to offices in more central areas 'to be close to employee housing, as this arrangement would save them time commuting' (PDF p.25, p.30).
- The office changes meaning: from 'the primary place of work' to 'a community space in which the main purpose is to meet and work with colleagues'; fewer assigned desks, more meeting rooms; one German company lets an employee who finds no free workstation leave after one hour (PDF p.28, p.30-31).
- The debate in the Member States (140 quotations): regulation first (43), then how to organise hybrid work (34), its definition (21), who pays the costs (19). Commuting appears only in the closing list of matters an agreement should cover: 'health and safety, work-life balance, working time, associated costs, commuting' (PDF p.23-26, p.39).
- Surveys cited: in a global survey of late 2020 nine in ten wanted to work remotely at least some of the time and one in four fully; executives expected employees on site between one and four days a week (PDF p.33, citing consultancies).
- In Italy the same arrangement is called 'Smart Working or Agile Work' (PDF p.19).

## Limits

Not a transport source: commuting appears in a few sentences as an expected benefit, with no data of its own. Information collected at the end of 2021, when work arrangements were still shaped by the pandemic. Figures are quoted from other works not opened here. Read by chapter: 14 of 46 pages; the chapters not read deal with definitions, national debates and company practices.
