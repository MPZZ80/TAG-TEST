## What it says

Commuting time of Canadian workers from 2011 to 2022. It notes that research shows long commutes increase the risk of negative health outcomes and reduce work-life balance.

## Data and quotes used

- In Canada in 2021, 926,000 workers took at least 60 minutes to reach work, down from 1.5 million in 2016; nearly two thirds of them (65.7%, 609,000 people) made the long commute by car (web page, 'A recent snapshot').
- Public transport commutes are much longer than car commutes: 42.9 minutes on average against 22.8 minutes by car, truck or van in 2021; average for all means 23.7 minutes (26.2 in 2016) (web page, historical trends).
- People with long commutes leave home earlier in the morning than those with short ones (web page, chart 2).
- Long commutes are most common in mining and oil (21.3%) and construction (13.6%), and in natural and applied sciences occupations (10.9%); least common in retail (4.0%) and education (4.3%) (web page).
- Men are more likely than women to have a long commute (8.5% against 5.4%) and have longer average commutes (25.6 against 21.6 minutes) (web page).
- 24.3% of workers usually worked from home in May 2021, against 7.4% in 2016 (web page).
- The agency states that research links long commutes to higher stress and poorer physical and mental health and to less time for other activities (web page, with notes to studies not opened here).

## Limits

Canada; the 2021 census was taken during pandemic restrictions, so times and counts are lower than normal and may not hold now. Commute time is by main means only, one way, self-reported. A long commute is defined by time, not by crossing between cities. Health statements rest on studies cited in notes and not opened.
