## What it says

The final report of the first Australian trial of Mobility as a Service: 92 employees of an insurance company in Sydney used an app with a journey planner, a mobility wallet and monthly subscription bundles from November 2019 to March 2020, when the pandemic stopped it. It is one of the very few MaaS trials that publishes what users actually did and said. Its findings cut against the idea that one app solves the problem: participants hardly used the journey planner, decided their mode before opening any app, preferred the tools they already had, and valued discounts and human support more than integration.

## Data and quotes used

- The report describes itself as the first trial worldwide with transparent reported quantitative evidence on MaaS (executive summary, PDF p.6; the sentence is cut in my extraction).
- "99% of all trips were taken without use of the Tripi application": the integrated app was almost not used to plan or book (PDF p.49).
- "Participants overwhelmingly already decide their mode of transport before using a journey planner to check the schedule (in the case of public transport), or Google Maps for its navigation features (in the case of driving)"; the choice rests on existing knowledge of destination and routes (PDF p.78).
- Many interviewees had never opened the trial app's journey planner after onboarding and had opened the app fewer than 10 times in four months; they preferred Google Maps and Apple Maps, 'mainly due to habit, but also design and functionality' (PDF p.77).
- Once public transport is chosen, people use a timetable app (TripView) that saves regular trips and is simpler than a multimodal planner; the trial app put timetables several taps away (PDF p.78).
- Exit survey (72 of 76 respondents): preferred journey planning app was Google Maps (average rank 2.12), then the timetable app TripView (2.44), then the trial app (2.67); Citymapper was among the least preferred (PDF p.122).
- When choosing a mode, 42% ranked convenience first, 28% cost, 22% safety and 7% sustainability (exit survey, PDF p.120).
- No interviewee could recall the CO2 figure shown for each trip; almost all said emissions matter in general but would not influence the choice of mode for a trip (PDF p.78).
- Participants 'would choose public transport only if it was already convenient'; one benchmark given was that public transport is considered only if it takes less than double the driving time (PDF p.76).
- 'Service levels (particularly of public transport) being far more important than potential cost savings' (summary of the interviews, PDF p.80).
- Participants were 'far less multimodal than expected' and cited 'more hassle [in] using an application' (PDF p.74, p.78).
- First and last mile: a 5 dollar incentive to use ride-hailing to and from public transport attracted very few participants; those who already walk or drive to the station showed little interest. The authors cite US pilots where such subsidised rides had low use because 'the additional transfer, wait time, insecurity to reach the bus/train provides an additional burden' (PDF p.35-36, p.60).
- Why 56% stayed on pay-as-you-go instead of a subscription: they do not travel often enough (many work from home 2-3 days a week), cannot estimate next month's travel, and dislike committing in advance to something they always had on demand (PDF p.72-73).
- The largest request: to be told beforehand how much each plan would save them; only a small minority calculated it, most relied on a 'hunch' (PDF p.73).
- Seeing all their travel spending in one statement was a surprise, 'Do I really spend this much on my travel?'; some called it 'bill shock'. One person discovered a bus route to work they did not know existed (PDF p.49, p.79).
- The onboarding in person, 15 to 60 minutes each, 'exceeded expectations' and participants valued support and feedback more than the app's functions; 'much more than an app and a set of subscription plans is needed' (PDF p.6, p.19, p.79).
- A missing provider weakens the whole offer: a cheaper ride-hailing competitor was not in the app, and many preferred it even after the discount (PDF p.49, p.74).
- Reliability of the alternative to the car matters: car sharing suffered from 'poor availability in some areas and, the more important concern, assurance of availability'; 'participants' own cars were viewed as an extension of their home' (PDF p.75, p.80).
- Uptake: 46% of participants were on a bundle by March 2020; 83% said they would buy the trial offering if it became available; 37% would be very likely to use the app after the trial and 27% unlikely (PDF p.25, p.51).
- Among those with tracked cars, bundle subscribers drove on average 284 km in February against 658 km for pay-as-you-go users, but people chose their own plan, so the authors do not claim the bundle caused the difference (PDF p.45, p.57).
- A group challenge to cut emissions produced a 1% increase, so no reward; the main obstacle to cutting emissions was 'the convenience of the private car' (43%), then public transport being slower (24%) or not available (19%) (PDF p.40, p.51).
- 'Without a (monetary) incentive, travellers appear to see very little value in MaaS in the presence of existing apps that are improving all the time' (PDF p.7).
- The authors could not find a business model without subsidy; they describe MaaS as slipping into the 'trough of disillusionment' (PDF p.7, p.61).

## Limits

92 volunteers, all employees of one insurance company, iPhone owners, working at offices next to major transport hubs in one city; 82% had daily access to a car. Not representative, and the authors say so. The trial ran about four and a half months, including the Christmas period, and was cut short by the pandemic. It is an urban trial in Sydney: no intercity commuting. People chose their own plan, so differences between groups are not effects. Pre-pandemic behaviour. The app was a white-label trip planner with known bugs, so low use partly reflects that product. Appendices B-E and G-H were not read.
