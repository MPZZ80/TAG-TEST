## What it says

A 2025 study for Japan's Digital Agency on how to measure demand for travel before introducing new mobility services (self-driving, on-demand) in three or four small areas. It compares how often residents want to go somewhere with how often they do. The unmet demand is for leisure and social trips, not for commuting, which people already make. For older people who will stop driving, only a door-to-door service is usable.

## Data and quotes used

- The report sorts regions into four types: large cities, suburban cities, regional cities and hilly or mountainous areas, by population density and by the share of commuters and students who depend on public transport (PDF p.16; working translation of the figure labels).
- Study commissioned by the Digital Agency of Japan under its 'Mobility Roadmap 2024'; about six months; method in steps: a survey for the broad picture, interviews to go deeper, a logic tree of the municipality's key project, a workshop to test it (PDF p.5-8, 20).
- Samples: a hilly rural area A (1,433 respondents), a regional city B (1,099), a suburban city C (number not given in the pages read); the survey measures, for shopping, medical visits, commuting or school and leisure, the gap between the wished and the actual frequency of trips (PDF p.21-23).
- Commuting shows no unmet demand: the gap between wished and actual frequency for commuting and school is zero or negative in every district (area A: -2.2% to +2.3%; city B: -2.0% to -19.8%; city C: -6.4% to -14.6%), i.e. people would rather commute less. For leisure the gap is large and positive (city B: +30.8% to +103.6%; city C: +40.0% to +74.3%) (tables, PDF p.38-40).
- Door to door as a condition: for an older relative with bad legs, after giving up the licence, JA 'Door to Door で移動してくれる移動サービスでないと使えない' (EN: only a mobility service that moves her door to door can be used). Another resident: the bus stop is within walking distance but 'I cannot get there because my legs are bad', and would not want to bother others when boarding (interviews in city B, PDF p.26, 39).
- Fear of the day the licence is given up: 'I will drive five more years; at the next renewal I will give it up, and I have no idea how I will move after that' (interview, city B, PDF p.26). Taxi is too expensive to be the answer (PDF p.39).
- Suburban city C (hilltop housing estates around a big city): steep slopes, few buses away from the station, no pavements, last train at 23:50 and a taxi queue after it with fares over 3,000 yen; commuters on public transport change at a monorail interchange; families who can work remotely find it good to live in, but 'when the children start university they find transport inconvenient and often leave'; much remote work means no places to meet (interviews with local groups, PDF p.27-28).
- Carers: the generation with children spends its days on work and on driving children and parents; the prospect of also driving parents after they give up the licence worries them (PDF p.25-26).
- Recommendation of the study: introduce a mobility service together with a reason to go out (a leisure or social destination), in the districts and time bands where the unmet demand is, mostly mornings (PDF p.38-40).

## Limits

Japan; small rural and suburban areas with ageing residents; aimed at self-driving and on-demand services, not at commuting between cities. Its value here is the method (wished against actual trips) and two points: commuting is not where people feel they travel too little, and door to door is a hard condition for people with reduced mobility. Small workshops; the tables give percentages without bases in the pages read. Only 29 of 227 pages read. Working translation from Japanese; quotes are residents' words as summarised on slides.
