## What it says

A 2025 report by Japan's transport ministry on digital technology in local transport. It says Japanese MaaS has not delivered one-stop access: each railway group builds its own app, other groups and taxis stay out, real-time search and one payment across operators are missing, and about 43% of MaaS projects since 2019 ended at the trial stage. The ministry's answer ('MaaS 2.0') is common standards, shared building blocks and open source led by the state, not more apps.

## Data and quotes used

- Of the MaaS-related projects between 2019 and 2024, about 43% ended as demonstrations without reaching operation, as of October 2024 (PDF p.17: "2019年～2024年の間で運用まで至らず実証終了したMaaS関連事業は約43%（2024年10月現在）"; working translation).
- Some demonstration projects end when subsidies and other support stop; likely causes are service quality problems, users that grow slowly and a lack of profitability (PDF p.17; working translation).
- Slide report by the Mobility Service Promotion Division of the Japanese transport ministry, April 2025, 164 pages (PDF p.1).
- Two families of problems: population decline and ageing (fewer users, driver shortage), and industry structure: JA 'サービス提供がサイロ化し全体最適化が難しい' (EN: service provision is siloed and optimising the whole is difficult), operators lack money and skills, and '業務オペレーションやデータ仕様の標準が乏しい' (EN: standards for operations and data formats are scarce) (PDF p.4, 6).
- Context figures: lines closed between fiscal 2000 and 2022: 1,160 km of rail and about 219,000 km of bus routes (PDF p.8). Bus operators in deficit: 99.6% in fiscal 2020, about 94% in 2021, about 87% in 2022; rail operators about 81% and 69% (PDF p.9). 45% of transport operators say they cannot renew ageing vehicles (PDF p.27).
- One-stop access is not achieved: JA 'MaaSの本来的価値である交通サービスへのアクセスのワンストップ化が実現していない' (EN: making access to transport services one-stop, which is the real value of MaaS, has not been achieved), because only some modes and operators join each service (PDF p.13, 15).
- Case, Fujisawa (app of one railway group): route search covers only rail and bus; no taxi; tickets and passes only for the group's own lines; the other railway groups, the national railway company's station and the taxis are outside (PDF p.13-14).
- Case, Nagoya (app of another railway group): search covers rail, bus and taxi, but 'real-time search that takes delays into account is not possible'; a taxi can be booked from the search only with partner companies; one payment for tickets across modes and operators is not possible (PDF p.15-16).
- Trials that do not last: of the MaaS-related projects started from 2019 to 2024, about 43% ended at the trial stage without going into operation (as of October 2024); some end when subsidies stop, because users do not grow and costs are not covered (PDF p.17).
- Two patterns in Japan: wide-area services by railway groups are well integrated in functions but weak in coverage of modes; small local services assembled from several software products cover more operators but are weak on ticketing and seamlessness (PDF p.19).
- Stated cause specific to Japan: mobility services are provided by many private operators, each at a high level on its own, which makes a seamless trip hard (PDF p.21).
- Foreign cases held up as advanced: Vienna (a shared base for booking, identification and payment built by the city's transport company, on which several apps run); Aachen region (run by the local public transport operators, with more accurate service information and park-and-ride routes combining car and public transport); Berlin (all public transport and sharing in one app, 885,000 downloads in five years from 2019); Switzerland's national railway app (PDF p.23-26).
- Current state as listed by the ministry: lack of links between means; information and booking systems split at the change; 'a complex and tiresome planning and booking process for the user'; interfaces hard to use and not designed for older people, people with disabilities or foreigners (PDF p.44).
- Method chosen for 'MaaS 2.0', from fiscal 2025: the state leads the creation of best practices, defines the areas where public and private should share results and standards, and publishes source code and know-how to lower the cost (PDF p.4, 34-40). Transport lags other sectors in digital change (PDF p.28-29).
- Second pass. Japanese MaaS services in operation by number of modes taking part (168 services): one mode 60% (100), two or three 26% (44), four or five 10% (17), six or more 4% (7). The ministry: services with several modes are 'less than half', and those are mostly rail and bus; in more than half of the services with six or more modes the operator is a council that includes the local authority (PDF p.46-47). Working translation.
- Seamlessness of the services with several operators: about one in ten has three or more of the five features (joint route search, booking, payment, one ticket, coordinated connections); 25% have none; 25% have joint payment only; 10% route search only (PDF p.48).
- Why one ticket across operators is rare: each vendor's ticket-validation interface is proprietary, so a digital ticket valid across operators with different equipment needs large development costs (PDF p.49, p.88).
- Apps: Japanese MaaS apps are rated lower in the App Store than international services such as Uber and Google Maps (January 2025); the ministry's explanation is too little capacity to invest in the interface, most of all where the service area is small (PDF p.51).
- Data: each ticketing system has its own data structure and user ID, so origin-destination data across modes cannot be obtained; timetable data (GTFS) and smart-card data write stops and trips differently and cannot be matched; demand-responsive services have no standard supply data (PDF p.59-61).
- Park and ride as a fare: a bus season ticket with a parking ticket and time-limited discounts were tested in Gunma and are 'not implemented at national level' (PDF p.52).
- United States, as summarised from a 2024 Department of Transportation document: mobility services are at level 1, information only (route search); no public body leads data standards; payment cannot be joined to route search because of limits on data sharing and the lack of real-time infrastructure (PDF p.130, p.133). Second-hand summary.
- United Kingdom, as summarised from the 2023 code of practice: the representative service is Citymapper, at level 1 (information); the government invested 92 million pounds in four 'future transport zones' to test apps that join planning, payment and tickets (West Midlands, Derby and Nottingham, West of England, Solent); one of the five measures is user protection, including making clear how to claim compensation for delays and cancellations and a refund (PDF p.160). Second-hand summary.
- United Kingdom acceptability research of 2020 (ten group interviews in five cities and eight depth interviews): people saw value in information 'on unfamiliar routes or when transport is delayed' and in one platform for modes and payment; barriers were the worry of not getting the cheapest price, no clear difference from existing planners and payment platforms, and personal data (PDF p.161). Second-hand summary, working translation.
- Netherlands, institute for transport policy analysis, 2018 (literature and focus groups in Amsterdam, Zwolle and rural areas): it is 'unlikely' that MaaS brings a large change to daily mobility or less car ownership within a few years; success depends on service quality (reliable, available anywhere, which is not realistic in rural areas), on added value (cost, convenience, choice, customisation) and on the person's travel habits (PDF p.162). Second-hand summary.
- MaaS Global (Whim), from the ministry's own interviews and press: about 10,000 monthly active users in Helsinki; plans at 59.7, 249 and 499 euro a month; 2022 revenue 3.8 million euro against a net loss of 14.89 million; staff from 120 in January 2020 to 28 in February 2024; bankruptcy filed in March 2024. Reasons given: an app cannot pass its fee on to users, so it needs volume and could not scale; subscriptions were cancelled after the invasion of Ukraine; the company bore the cost and time of adapting operators' systems; high fees blocked sales outside the EU. The funding figure is given as 162 million dollars, or 64.2 million euro according to Crunchbase, which differs from other sources (PDF p.163).
- European data-space project deployEMDS: about 8 million euro, November 2023 to October 2026, 16 use cases in 9 cities. For commuting: Lisbon (45% of trips in the city and 58.9% in the metropolitan area by car; packages for employers to cut car use for work and commuting) and Ile-de-France (a platform for employers' mobility credits, because 'dependence on the private car for commuting remains high' despite subsidies and existing apps have 'limited coverage and integration'). Milan: 280 bus lines and 10,800 daily runs across provinces with no single system to plan them. Budapest's app has about 120,000 monthly active users (PDF p.139-152).
- Tampere, in the same project: static public transport data are available, but 'real-time data has no occupancy information and historical data cannot be accessed' (PDF p.152).

## Limits

A ministry slide deck that argues for its own programme ('MaaS 2.0'). Most of it concerns rural and local transport in Japan, a context unlike intercity commuting in Europe; used for the mechanisms of failed integration. The foreign cases in the appendix are the ministry's summaries of other documents and are second-hand: check the originals before citing them. Many charts did not convert; the figures above come from the text of the slides. Working translation from Japanese.
