## Summary

1. **Commuters use one means of transport, and mostly the car.** In Italy 84.6% of commuters always use a single means, and only 4.8% combine public and private means (F33). The same pattern holds in Madrid, Switzerland and the EU (F7, F34). Combining is more common on longer trips and on trips that leave the municipality, which is where this project looks (F35). These counts leave out the walks at both ends, which surveys record badly (F62). Asked why, people answer "the simplest", and one leg in six by car is described as having no alternative (F63).
2. **The intercity commuter is a small group, and train commuters are the long-distance ones.** In Italy 4% of workers and students travel more than 50 km to work or study, and 2.2% of all trips exceed 50 km (F36). In Germany 1 to 2% of adults commute over 100 km (F37). In Ghent three in four regular train commuters travel more than 45 minutes, and they are the least satisfied commuters because of the length of the trip, not because of the train (F54). No official statistic describes this group door to door. The one study that does, on a Chinese high-speed line, finds that getting from the arrival station to the workplace takes longer than the train ride (F53).
3. **Working from home goes with longer commutes.** In Germany workers who can work from home live 22 km from work against 15 km, and the distance grows with the days at home (F38). In Japan half of those who commute 90 minutes or more telework, against 13.5% of those under 30 minutes (F39). Presence at the workplace concentrates in the middle of the week (F40). Season tickets built on five days a week fit badly (F41), and people who cannot predict their month do not buy one (F67).
4. **In a disruption, what people lack is what to do next and an honest reason.** Only 37% of delayed British rail passengers were satisfied with the information on alternatives and connections (F20). App reviews in four countries describe the same thing from inside: alternatives that cannot be travelled, delay messages that are late or wrong in both directions, and the status of the current trip lost when looking for another (F42, F43). Where another line runs nearby most passengers reroute by themselves; a simulation puts the cost of knowing nothing at two hours, against ten minutes with complete information at the start (F57). One regular traveller's diaries show the same from inside: channels that contradict each other, forecasts he does not believe, and information that comes after the alternative has left (F73).
5. **People decide how to travel before they open an app, and many do not decide at all.** In the Sydney trial 99% of trips were made without the integrated app, and participants had already chosen the mode before checking a timetable (F44). Regular travellers use an app to check their usual trip at a glance: saved trips, platform, delay (F45). Habit is strong enough to measure: being a habitual driver cuts the odds of commuting by any other means by about nine tenths, and for many commuters there is one usable option, not a set (F56). Shown the travel times, people take a route that is good enough, not the fastest (F5).
6. **The service matters more than the tool.** Italians name punctuality as the first thing that would make them use public transport more (F46). Users blame apps for the failures of the service behind them (F47). Focus groups shown a combined-travel app call it "the icing on an imaginary cake" (F31), and researchers rank the needs in the same order: services that work together, then information, then payment (F61). What was a hypothesis in the first version of this file is now one of the best supported findings.
7. **A change between two services costs far more than its minutes**, and planners propose the tightest change, which regular travellers do not trust (F16, F48). Each added service multiplies the weak points: travellers add "wiggle room", describe a "wait time dilemma", and prefer to keep one leg under their own control (F9). An experienced traveller keeps his own rules and his own statistics of connections made (F72), and loses his connections on the bus or tram at either end, where nobody protects them (F74).
8. **"Everything in one app" has not found its users.** Demand is unproven at scale, most pilots ended, and the Italian national programme reached about 59,000 users with 56.9 million euros (F27 to F30, F49). The studies in its favour ask people what they would choose, in cities (F58). In the one long trial with paying customers, what changed behaviour was paying per use and seeing every option each time, more than having everything in one place (F59). The authors of the Sydney trial ended by asking whether they had "overestimated what passengers really want" (F76).
9. **Integration is blocked by rules and interests more than by technology.** The European rules proposed in 2026 leave urban transport out, so the first and last legs stay outside the single ticket; real-time data were optional until 2024; incumbents hold 80 to 90% of online rail sales (F25, F26, F50). No journey planner covers planning, live data and tickets together: city planners have the live data, operators have the tickets (F60). In Italy the supply itself is split among about 700 road public transport firms and 41 regional rail contracts, and the regulator writes of a structural lack of data (F70). The Commission's own assessment expects little change and gives passengers no complaint of their own (F71). On the motorway and on the bus the traveller has less information and fewer rights than on the train (F69).
10. **The evidence is still weakest where the brief is most specific:** the door-to-door journey of someone who commutes between cities, leg by leg, and what they decide each day. Three questions have no material at all (Q5, Q7, Q12) and nine have only indirect evidence. The 18 papers added in the evening are urban, or from outside Europe, or about a service described on paper, and the pages read at night add one enthusiast's diaries, not commuters' accounts.

## Evidence against the brief's assumptions

The brief assumes that commuters combine modes and that fragmented information is the core problem. The complete reading makes the first assumption weaker and changes the second.

- **Few commuters combine modes** (F6, F33, F34), and people who do often use a means they dislike (F51).
- **Routine trips need little information.** Frequent travellers want to know about deviations from the normal, not how to get there (F45). For regular trips "users have established habits, routes and destinations" (F44). Research on travel information says that people look for information about a way of travelling they already consider, not the other way round (F61).
- **An integrated planner is hardly used even when it is free and subsidised** (F44, F49).
- **Most people who book combined journeys say it is easy**, and the Commission's own estimate of what better ticketing could add to rail is 1.7% (F14, F15).
- **Reliability of the service comes before information** (F46, F47, F31). People say they would not pay for easier access to services that do not meet their needs (F58).
- **Habit.** 22% of Europeans say they find it hard to change habits (F13); people have a main means and a predetermined fallback (F44); temporary incentives do not last (F52); habitual drivers almost never commute by another means, whatever the service near them (F56).
- **Many commuters do not choose.** The people who kept using public transport in the pandemic were mostly those with no alternative, and researchers write of mode "use", not mode "choice" (F56). The brief speaks of commuters who evaluate options every day.
- **The reason given for not combining is that it is not needed.** In the Commission's survey 29% of those who never booked a combined journey say "It was not needed" and 4% that they could not buy the tickets in one place (F71). Asked why they used a means, people say it is the simplest (F63).
- **What does support the brief:** in a disruption the information on what to do next is missing or wrong (F20, F42, F43), and what passengers know decides how much time they lose (F57); tickets do not carry over between services (F50); travellers describe being tired of moving between apps (F29); every added service multiplies the reasons not to combine (F9); and no planner covers planning, live data and tickets together (F60). A regular traveller's diaries describe the contradiction between channels in detail, with the limit that he is one person and not a daily commuter (F73).

### Who (Q1–Q8)

**Q1. Who commutes between cities, why, and what does the journey look like door to door?** Partial.

- **F1.** In the EU-27 in 2019, 61.3% of workers reached work in less than 30 minutes, 26.3% in 30 to 59 minutes and 8.1% in 60 minutes or more. The average was 25 minutes in the EU and 21 in Italy, among the shortest. Commuting time grows with education. Source: [GR-001](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-001_eurostat_2020_commuting-time.md). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: medium.
- **F2.** In Italy in 2019, 12 million employed people and 3.5 million students travelled outside their municipality every day; 900,000 people took a train daily and 1 million several times a week. In Lombardy more than two employed people in three work in another municipality. In the EU 12.5 million people commuted to another region in 2022, mostly around capitals (this last figure was read through an automatic page summary). Sources: [GR-023](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-023_istat_2020_spostamenti-sul-territorio-prima-del-covid.md) (PDF p.1, p.3), [GR-002](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-002_eurostat_2023_interregional-commuters.md). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: medium.
- **F36.** Distance from home to work or study in Italy: 4% travel more than 50 km, 37% between 10 and 50 km, 46% between 1 and 10 km. In Europe 7% travel more than 50 km. Trips over 50 km are 2.2% of all trips in Italy (2.6% in 2019); the average trip outside the municipality is 25.1 km and 32 minutes. Sources: [GR-017](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-017_ipsos-europ-assistance_2025_mobility-barometer.md) (PDF p.12, read from the chart image), [GR-008](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-008_isfort_2025_rapporto-mobilita-22-sintesi.md) (PDF p.4–6). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: medium (one commercial survey and one research institute, different measures that agree).
- **F37.** In Germany, with a threshold of 100 km, 1 to 2% of adults are long-distance commuters, some only occasionally; 45% of them cover 100 to 250 km; most go by car and long-distance rail also plays a role. The survey calls the trip "keine wirkliche Privatreise, keine echte Geschäftsreise" (not really a private trip, not a true business trip) and says it is hard to see in a one-day diary. 23% of long-distance train trips are commutes. Source: [GR-041](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-041_bmv-infas_2025_mobilitaet-in-deutschland-2023.md) (long report p.19, p.111, p.229). Confidence: medium.
- **F53.** Where the journey has been followed door to door, the leg at the work end is the long one. Among 288 people who commute by high-speed train from Suzhou to Shanghai (about 30 minutes on the train), the trip from home to the station takes under 30 minutes for 69% and 55% make it by car; the trip from the arrival station to the workplace takes over 30 minutes for 72% and over 60 minutes for 23%, and 77% make it by public transport. The authors conclude that the time a fast train saves "does not necessarily result in reduced commuting time when the door-to-door journey (both the first and the last mile) is taken into account". The same asymmetry appears among suburban commuters into Dhaka (1.25 km to the stop and 2.39 km from it, more for rail users than for bus users) and in Orlando, where the car is used almost only between home and the stop (90% of drive-alone trips to a stop start from home). Sources: [AP-025](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-025_wang_2020_exploring-physical-mental-health.md) (table 1, PDF p.6; p.2), [AP-008](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-008_rahman_2022_first-last-mile-public.md) (tables 2 and 3, PDF p.6), [AP-010](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-010_azimi_2020_mode-choice-behavior-access.md) (table 4, PDF p.7). Leg: first mile, last mile. Confidence: high for the pattern in the places studied (two grade A studies and one grade B agree). None is European and only the first concerns commuting between cities, so whether it holds for an Italian intercity commuter is open.
- **F54.** Train commuters are the long-distance commuters, and their low satisfaction comes from the length of the trip more than from the train. In Ghent 73.4% of people who mostly or always commute by train travel more than 45 minutes; they are the least satisfied group (2.74 on a scale of 5, against about 4.2 for short walks and bike rides), and satisfaction falls almost in a straight line from 4.41 for commutes up to 10 minutes to 2.60 over 45. With duration held constant the train makes no difference, and only frequent car use keeps a negative effect of its own. In urban England a longer distance to work raises the odds of commuting by train, as does a household income over 60,000 pounds. Sources: [AP-006](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-006_vos_2022_does-commute-duration-attenuate.md) (tables 4 and 5, PDF p.4–7), [AP-027](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-027_keyes_2018_changing-influences-commuting-mode.md) (table 4, PDF p.7). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: medium (both studies link the train with distance; the result on satisfaction rests on one grade A study; urban samples of 2017 and 2009–2010).
- **F55.** A fast train turns people who would have moved home, or not taken the job, into commuters, and the operators do not plan for them. In the Suzhou–Shanghai study 74% became long-distance commuters after the high-speed line opened; 76% do it for a better-paid job and 80% for their career, while 47% say it gives a better quality of life; 38% report worse physical health since they started and 30% better, and the new commuters fare worse than those who already commuted by slower means. The authors call it "a substitute for migration" and write that it "is largely a users' aspiration and struggle rather than the intention of HSR operators", who "welcome business trips and discourage commuting"; no season ticket exists on those trains. Source: [AP-025](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-025_wang_2020_exploring-physical-mental-health.md) (PDF p.8, p.12–13). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: low (one grade B study, China, a convenience sample; used for the mechanism). It echoes the German description of the long commute as neither a private nor a business trip (F37).
- Still open: the door-to-door journey itself in Europe. Statistics assign each trip one main means and one distance band (F34), so the legs are invisible, and the one study that shows them is Chinese (F53).

**Q2. How do commuters differ by frequency?** Partial, much better covered than before.

- **F3.** Working from home every day fell from 24.6% of the employed in Italy in 2020 to 4% in 2024, while remote workers stabilised at just over 3.5 million (570,000 in 2019): hybrid work replaced full remote work. 64% of Italian workers never work from home; those who do average 0.9 days a week. Shares with some home working: 42% of German workers have the option, 43% of Dutch workers do at least an hour a week, 24.6% of Japanese employees telework. Sources: [GR-008](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-008_isfort_2025_rapporto-mobilita-22-sintesi.md) (PDF p.15), [GR-017](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-017_ipsos-europ-assistance_2025_mobility-barometer.md) (PDF p.13), [GR-041](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-041_bmv-infas_2025_mobilitaet-in-deutschland-2023.md) (p.211), [GR-043](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-043_kim_2024_kerncijfers-mobiliteit-2024.md) (PDF p.19), [GR-048](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-048_mlit_2025_telework-jinkou-jittai-chousa-r6.md) (PDF p.14). Confidence: high.
- **F4.** Commuting has not returned to its level before the pandemic: commute trips in Germany fell 13% against 2017 although employment grew; commuting distance in the Netherlands is 16% below 2019; in the US about 9 million fewer workers leave home in the morning core hours; Italian regional rail lost 11% of passengers from 2019 to 2024, which the regulator links to commuters travelling fewer days a week. Sources: [GR-041](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-041_bmv-infas_2025_mobilitaet-in-deutschland-2023.md) (p.68), [GR-043](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-043_kim_2024_kerncijfers-mobiliteit-2024.md) (PDF p.19), [GR-031](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-031_us-census-bureau_2024_commuting-in-the-us-2022.md) (PDF p.7), [GR-008](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-008_isfort_2025_rapporto-mobilita-22-sintesi.md) (PDF p.4), [GR-003](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-003_art_2025_relazione-annuale.md). Confidence: high.
- **F38.** In Germany the distance to the workplace is 15 km for workers without home office and 22 km with it, and it grows with the days at home: 18 km for less than one day a week, 20 for one to two, 24 for two to three, 26 for three or more. The survey reads this as home office being used "to avoid especially long commutes" and adds that longer distances are probably accepted more easily when there are fewer trips. In Switzerland workers who can work at home make fewer but longer work trips. Sources: [GR-041](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-041_bmv-infas_2025_mobilitaet-in-deutschland-2023.md) (table 55, p.213–214), [GR-035](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-035_bfs-are_2023_mikrozensus-mobilitaet-verkehr-2021.md) (PDF p.48). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: high (two official surveys agree).
- **F39.** In Japan the share of employees who telework rises with the one-way commute: 13.5% under 30 minutes, 29.2% for 30 to 60, 43.3% for 60 to 90, 50.6% for 90 minutes or more. It is 42.7% among rail and bus commuters and 13.2% among car commuters. In the Madrid region 41.5% of teleworkers in the outer ring work from home five days a week, against 20.3% in central Madrid; the report reads it as "a stable solution to avoid long trips". Sources: [GR-048](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-048_mlit_2025_telework-jinkou-jittai-chousa-r6.md) (PDF p.14–15, chart images), [GR-045](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-045_crtm_2025_encuesta-sintetica-movilidad-madrid-2024.md). Confidence: high. None of these surveys can say which comes first, the long commute or the home working.
- The surveys cannot say which comes first, but one Italian study reports the direction: in Sicily telework brought fewer trips a week and more distance, "because employees were more willing to commute farther considering they did not commute as often". Source: [AP-012](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-012_zarabi_2023_understanding-travel-mode-choice.md) (table 2, PDF p.11; one study, known through this review). Confidence: low.
- **F40.** Presence concentrates in the middle of the week. In Madrid telework is highest on Monday and Friday (about 21%) and lowest on Thursday (16.9%). In Germany almost 10 million fewer commute trips are made on an average Friday than in 2017, and the smallest fall is on Wednesday. Teleworkers in Japan average 2.1 days a week, and most would like more. Sources: [GR-045](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-045_crtm_2025_encuesta-sintetica-movilidad-madrid-2024.md) (PDF p.55), [GR-041](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-041_bmv-infas_2025_mobilitaet-in-deutschland-2023.md) (p.68–69), [GR-048](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-048_mlit_2025_telework-jinkou-jittai-chousa-r6.md) (PDF p.19, p.23). Confidence: medium.
- **F41.** Passes built on five days a week fit hybrid commuters badly. Great Britain introduced a ticket for any 8 days in 28 in 2021, because "the idea of travelling 5 days a week to the office is fast becoming a relic of the past"; a commentator called it "a bulk purchase of tickets", not a season ticket. In the Sydney trial 56% stayed on pay-as-you-go because they travel too little, cannot estimate next month's travel and dislike committing in advance. ISFORT warns that remote work can reduce the demand for season tickets. Participants in the Gothenburg trial of 2013–2014 said the same of the monthly pass before hybrid work spread: "It matters if you are at home for 3 days and get to pay for those days anyhow". An English commuter who travels one or two days a week parks in the street because "the station parking is crazily expensive". Sources: [AP-019](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-019_stromberg_2018_inviting-travelers-smorgasbord-sustainable.md) (PDF p.11), [AP-015](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-015_mcilroy_2023_this-where-public-transport.md) (table 2, PDF p.8), [GR-052](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-052_govuk_2021_flexible-season-tickets-announcement.md), [AR-009](Sources/Articles/AR-009_personnel-today_2021_flexible-rail-tickets-hybrid.md), [GR-037](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-037_imove-australia_2021_sydney-maas-trial-final-report.md) (PDF p.72–73), [GR-008](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-008_isfort_2025_rapporto-mobilita-22-sintesi.md) (PDF p.43). Confidence: medium.
- **F67.** A pass assumes that people can predict their travel, and with work from home they cannot. In Switzerland holders of a public transport pass fell from 57% of adults in 2015 to 53% in 2021, and the statistical office links it to people who "because of more work from home no longer bought a pass"; yet 61.6% of those who can work at home hold one, against 45.7% of the others. In Sydney the participants who never took a monthly plan said they could not estimate their own travel (20%) or did not use the discounted means (21%), and in interviews named working from home, travel that changes from month to month and not wanting to commit for a month. Sources: [GR-035](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-035_bfs-are_2023_mikrozensus-mobilitaet-verkehr-2021.md) (PDF p.17, p.62), [GR-037](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-037_imove-australia_2021_sydney-maas-trial-final-report.md) (PDF p.165, p.188, p.228). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: medium (two grade B sources agree). It supports F41 from the user's side.
- **F65.** Telework has settled below its peak, and the days at home move daily errands from the workplace area to the home area. In Japan the share of employees who teleworked in the last year fell from 21.4% in fiscal 2021 to 15.6% in fiscal 2024 (capital area 36.2% to 27.2%), which the ministry reads as settling above the level before the pandemic; among employee teleworkers the share who intend to continue fell from 89.4% to 63.7%. On days at home food and daily shopping near home increased for 37.8% of teleworkers and near the workplace for 14.1%; eating out near the workplace decreased for 24.8%. About one teleworker in five also works "mobile", a category that includes trains. Source: [GR-048](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-048_mlit_2025_telework-jinkou-jittai-chousa-r6.md) (PDF p.13, p.18, p.22, p.27–34, read from page images). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: low (one grade B source, Japan). It matters here because errands are often chained to the commute: on a hybrid week the chain changes with the day.
- Still open: which days Italian intercity commuters travel, who decides them, and what they buy.

**Q3. How does familiarity with a route change planning?** Partial.

- **F5.** People take a route that is good enough, not the fastest. In a computer experiment in Lyon, 353 people who were shown the travel time of three car routes chose the fastest in 60.5% of 3,664 choices, and only 9.5% of them chose it every time. The average participant treated routes up to about 31% slower than the fastest as equivalent and then chose among them on other grounds, first of all how direct the route looks. A difference is weighed in proportion to the length of the trip, not in minutes. A route presented as the fastest is chosen about 10 points more often than the next even when the difference is small. Field studies with GPS traces that the paper cites find the fastest route on 25 to 75% of real trips. Source: [AP-022](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-022_ramirez_2020_travel-time-bounded-rationality.md) (table 2, PDF p.9; p.12–13, p.16). Leg: main leg, by car. Confidence: low (one grade B study: a game on a screen, two thirds of the players students, nothing at stake; the field studies are cited at second hand).
- **F45.** Frequent travellers "may have little need for navigation information, but a greater interest in learning about variations from the expected performance". Regular users of railway apps say the same in their own words: saved trips are "the only reason for using the app instead of google maps"; "I'm not a tourist"; in the morning they want the platform "without an extra action". When updates removed saved trips or hid the platform, a quarter of the Dutch sample and a fifth of the Swiss one complained, and both operators reversed the change. A British commuter asks for a standing alert on the two trains he takes every day. Research reviewed in 2019 says the same: "Journey situations that are familiar and predictable are associated with low demand for use of (formal) information services", and demand "is especially high at times of transport service disruption". Sources: [AP-003](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-003_lyons_2019_importance-user-perspective-evolution.md) (PDF p.11), [GR-040](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-040_trb_2003_tcrp-92-traveler-information.md) (PDF p.28), [OR-008](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-008_apple-app-store_2026_ns-app-nl.md), [OR-014](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-014_apple-app-store_2026_sbb-mobile-ch.md), [OR-005](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-005_apple-app-store_2026_trainline-gb.md) (2026-09-18). Leg: main leg, transfer. Confidence: medium (a research review and many independent reviews in three countries; the review is from 2003).

**Q4. What constraints shape the journey?** Weak.

- Chains of activities and people in care push toward the car: people with many daily activities or little flexibility on arrival time are less likely to use public transport, and having a person in care lowers bus commuting ([AP-001](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-001_goransson_2023_factors-that-make-public.md), PDF p.9). Women's trips are more complex and cost more when several tickets are needed ([GR-009](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-009_oecd-itf_2021_integrating-public-transport-into-maas.md), PDF p.13). Transport takes 10.8% of the Italian household budget ([GR-004](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-004_legambiente_2025_pendolaria-20-edizione.md), PDF p.50). Interviewees in three English cities value the car for the same constraints: carrying things, family, emergencies and not depending on a timetable ([AP-004](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-004_alyavina_2020_mobility-as-service-sustainable.md), PDF p.9). Confidence: low. A paper on acceptable travel time ([AP-020](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-020_milakis_2018_me-it-always-like.md)) was not obtained: payment is needed.

**Q5. Which options does each commuter have on each leg?** Open. Evidence on supply exists (half of Germans have poor public transport at home, F46), not on what one commuter can choose from on each leg. A model of English commuters and a review of commuting in the pandemic suggest that for many there is one usable option, not a set (F56).

**Q6. Who combines several modes and who uses one?** Partial, well covered.

- **F6.** In Italy trips made with a combination of motorised means were 2.8% of motorised trips in the first half of 2025, 3.2% in 2024 and 6.5% in 2019; higher in the North-West and in large cities. ISFORT concludes that such behaviour "remains entirely marginal: the average user tends to prefer a single mode, often out of habit or lack of information". Source: [GR-008](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-008_isfort_2025_rapporto-mobilita-22-sintesi.md) (PDF p.8–9, p.48). Confidence: medium. The measure may not count a walk or a bike ride to the station.
- **F33.** In Italy in 2017, 84.6% of commuters always used one single means. More than one means: 11.2% of the employed and 34.4% of students. Public and private combined: 4.8% of commuters, 4.1% of the employed; highest in the outskirts of metropolitan areas (6.3%). Among the employed 73.7% use only private means. Source: [GR-024](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-024_istat_2018_spostamenti-quotidiani-e-nuove-forme-di-mobilita.md) (PDF p.1, p.5, p.7). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: medium (official statistics, 2017).
- **F7.** In the Madrid region 91.3% of trips have one leg; combining a private vehicle with another means is 0.1% of trips. In Switzerland 82% of trips have one leg, walking plus public transport is a good 9%, and car or bike plus public transport are each under 1%. A 2015 EU study found 78% use one mode for their most frequent trip. Sources: [GR-045](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-045_crtm_2025_encuesta-sintetica-movilidad-madrid-2024.md) (PDF p.42–43), [GR-035](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-035_bfs-are_2023_mikrozensus-mobilitaet-verkehr-2021.md) (PDF p.26, p.31), [GR-009](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-009_oecd-itf_2021_integrating-public-transport-into-maas.md) (PDF p.14). Confidence: high.
- **F35.** Combining rises with distance. In Italy most trips made with more than one means are by people who leave their municipality (68.7%, my reading of the sentence). In Madrid public transport takes 70.9% of trips of 61 to 90 minutes against 2.4% under 10 minutes. In Switzerland rail is the main means for 23% of outings over 50 km. In England 58% of public transport trips use more than one mode and 64% of those start on foot. In the Netherlands the multimodal group that uses the train makes twice as many trip segments as car users and is young, urban and often without a car. Sources: [GR-024](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-024_istat_2018_spostamenti-quotidiani-e-nuove-forme-di-mobilita.md) (PDF p.7), [GR-045](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-045_crtm_2025_encuesta-sintetica-movilidad-madrid-2024.md) (table 23), [GR-035](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-035_bfs-are_2023_mikrozensus-mobilitaet-verkehr-2021.md) (PDF p.29), [GR-030](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-030_dft_2025_national-travel-survey-2024.md), [AP-024](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-024_an_2022_multimodal-travel-behaviour-attitudes.md) (table 3). Confidence: medium.
- **F34.** Official statistics hide combined trips. Germany assigns a trip to one main means by a fixed order; France and the US record only the main means; Australia collects up to three methods and publishes one; Eurostat does not report trips between cities. Only the Swiss, Dutch and Madrid surveys count legs. Sources: [GR-041](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-041_bmv-infas_2025_mobilitaet-in-deutschland-2023.md) (short report p.34), [GR-042](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-042_sdes_2023_mobilite-locale-et-longue-distance-des-francais.md) (PDF p.8), [GR-031](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-031_us-census-bureau_2024_commuting-in-the-us-2022.md), [GR-034](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-034_abs_2022_australias-journey-to-work.md), [GR-016](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-016_eurostat_2021_passenger-mobility-statistics.md). Confidence: high. This is why the project's user is hard to count.
- **F62.** The first and last legs on foot are the part of the journey that surveys record worst. In Germany more than one means is named for only 13% of trips and almost all of these include a walk: "intermodal trips can in essence be equated with trips that have at least one walking leg". A walking leg is reported for 52% of public transport trips, and the report says they are clearly under-reported because respondents "often do not notice them or do not think them worth reporting"; where legs were recorded there are 1.3 walking legs per trip, one kilometre and 10 minutes against an average trip of 28 minutes. In Madrid a change made on foot is by definition not a stage, and a park-and-ride trip is counted as public transport, so the 91.3% of one-stage trips in F7 leaves out every walk to and from a stop. In Switzerland, where every leg is recorded, a work trip has 1.5 legs and walking is 2% of the distance, 17% of the time and 33% of the legs. Sources: [GR-041](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-041_bmv-infas_2025_mobilitaet-in-deutschland-2023.md) (PDF p.52, p.147–148), [GR-045](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-045_crtm_2025_encuesta-sintetica-movilidad-madrid-2024.md) (PDF p.29; interviewer instructions p.17), [GR-035](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-035_bfs-are_2023_mikrozensus-mobilitaet-verkehr-2021.md) (PDF p.49). Leg: first and last mile. Confidence: medium (three grade B surveys that agree). The share of one-means trips in F7 and F33 is therefore an upper bound.
- **F51.** People who mix modes often use one they dislike. In the Netherlands the car is the best liked means in every group, including the multimodal train users (car 18.3, train 10.2 on the same scale); the odds of using a disliked means are 6 to 10 times higher for multimodal public transport users than for car users; the authors write that "multimodal individuals in general use modes they dislike" and may move toward the car. In Ghent only 17.9% of public transport users prefer it, and people who travel with their preferred means are more satisfied whatever it is. Sources: [AP-024](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-024_an_2022_multimodal-travel-behaviour-attitudes.md) (PDF p.7–10), [AP-018](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-018_vos_2018_people-travel-their-preferred.md) (PDF p.15–18). Confidence: medium (two studies; one is on leisure trips in one city).
- Still open: why the Italian share fell after 2019, and what holds a combination together for those who keep it.

**Q7. Who else is involved in the travel decisions?** Weak, no longer open. Employers set the days (F40, F66); a Dutch reward app raised the worry that the employer could see the trips ([OR-016](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-016_apple-app-store_2026_reiswijs-nl.md)); relatives with a car are the backup at both ends of the journey (F74).

- **F66.** Who decides the office days is the open issue of hybrid work, and the usual rule is one to three days in the office. In 80 examples of hybrid work in organisations of the 27 Member States, 40 of 107 policy elements fix a number of days in the office, 15 leave the place to the employee and 5 to the team; "One critical issue in hybrid work is who – the employer or the employee – is authorised to decide how much time is spent teleworking and how much is spent in the office". Eliminating the commute is 2 of 27 stated reasons for introducing hybrid work. Some organisations opened hubs "in more remote areas for those employees who lived further away". Source: [GR-014](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-014_eurofound_2023_hybrid-work-in-europe.md) (PDF p.13, p.28–31). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: low (one grade B source; examples collected at the end of 2021, when most companies had little experience).

**Q8. Which groups experience the most friction?** Partial.

- **F8.** Commuters are the least satisfied rail passengers in Great Britain: 41% satisfied with how a delay was handled against 55% of leisure travellers; value for money 49% against 67%. Source: [GR-051](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-051_transport-focus_2026_rail-customer-experience-survey.md). Leg: main leg. Confidence: medium.
- **F9.** Combining services multiplies the weak points. In online focus groups with 146 residents of the Solent area in England, the same four barriers came first in cities, suburbs and villages: timetables that do not meet; the time lost, including the "wiggle room" people add; "critical reliability" ("the risk that one trip is delayed and then you miss the next part of your journey or is even cancelled and you are left in the middle of nowhere"); and tickets whose costs stack. Six people described a "wait time dilemma": enough time for the change, but not so much that one is "left standing in the cold/rain/dark for ages". One participant's rule is "to have one mode within your control and the other outside of your control": walking, a scooter or a car plus one train, and never two services in a row. Another: "any reasons not to use public transport are squared, not doubled, when using multiple". Planning across several websites was named as a barrier only where there are options to plan; no rural participant raised it. Source: [AP-015](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-015_mcilroy_2023_this-where-public-transport.md) (PDF p.7–12). Leg: transfer, first and last mile. Confidence: medium (one grade A study; qualitative, one English region). It agrees with F16 and F48.
- **F10.** On some Italian commuter lines the service itself fails often: on the Roma Nord line a commuters' committee counted more than 8,000 cancelled runs in ten months of 2025; on the former Circumvesuviana a student whose train should arrive at 7:51 arrives between 8:10 and 8:20 and takes an earlier train on exam days. Source: [GR-004](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-004_legambiente_2025_pendolaria-20-edizione.md) (PDF p.40). Confidence: low (advocacy report). The regulator's figures are far better (punctuality 89 to 97%) but count a train as late only after 5 minutes at destination and do not count a cancelled train replaced within half an hour ([GR-003](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-003_art_2025_relazione-annuale.md), PDF p.122, p.216).
- Other groups named: people in peripheral areas, who spend more on a car or more time on public transport ([GR-010](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-010_egum_2024_public-transport-shared-mobility.md), PDF p.8); people with reduced mobility, for whom only a door-to-door service is usable ([GR-046](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-046_digital-agency-japan_2025_idou-juyou-no-haaku-saishu-houkokusho.md), PDF p.26).

### Planning before leaving (Q9–Q12)

**Q9. How and when do people decide how to travel?** Weak, no longer open.

- **F44.** The means is decided before any tool is opened. In the Sydney trial "participants overwhelmingly already decide their mode of transport before using a journey planner to check the schedule"; 99% of trips were made without the trial app; many never opened its planner. British research found that people have a primary means and fall back on a predetermined default. For routine trips "users have established habits, routes and destinations". A study of long-distance public transport trips found "travel behaviour having a stronger impact on information use than vice versa" (F61). One trial points the other way: an app that listed every option each time made participants reconsider (F59). Sources: [AP-003](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-003_lyons_2019_importance-user-perspective-evolution.md) (PDF p.11), [GR-037](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-037_imove-australia_2021_sydney-maas-trial-final-report.md) (PDF p.49, p.77–78), [GR-040](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-040_trb_2003_tcrp-92-traveler-information.md) (PDF p.23), [GR-009](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-009_oecd-itf_2021_integrating-public-transport-into-maas.md) (PDF p.14), [AP-017](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-017_vos_2017_travel-satisfaction-revisited-pivotal.md) (PDF p.12). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: medium (urban commuters in Sydney, a 2003 review, an expert round table).
- Still open: when the decision is made on a given day, and whether for the whole journey or leg by leg.

**Q10. Routine trip compared with an unusual one?** Weak. For an unfamiliar trip people need an itinerary; for a familiar one, the deviations (F45). Planners are praised for unknown places: "Everyone is a local with this" ([OR-003](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-003_apple-app-store_2026_citymapper-gb.md)). Nothing describes how a commuter prepares for an unusual day.

**Q11. What information do people look for before leaving, and where?** Partial.

- **F11.** Before going to the station 38% of British rail passengers looked for nothing and "just turned up"; 43% checked live departures for punctuality or platform; 68% of those who looked used an app. What a commuter can still change before leaving is the means, the route and the departure time, and what they want is news of disruptions and delays. Vendors build for the repeated trip (saved searches, favourite routes, commuter sections). Sources: [GR-051](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-051_transport-focus_2026_rail-customer-experience-survey.md) (section 5), [GR-040](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-040_trb_2003_tcrp-92-traveler-information.md) (table 1), [PR-009](Sources/Products/PR-009_trenitalia_2026_trenitalia-app.md), [PR-007](Sources/Products/PR-007_db-fernverkehr_2026_db-navigator.md), [PR-014](Sources/Products/PR-014_sbb_2026_sbb-mobile.md). Confidence: medium.
- People use live data to decide when to leave home: a Dutch reviewer had learned to wait at home watching the bus position; an Italian forum user checked delays "to know when to go to the station"; a FlixBus customer left later after a delay notice and found the bus on time. Sources: [OR-015](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-015_apple-app-store_2026_9292-nl.md), [OF-001](Sources/Online_Forums/OF-001_ferrovie-it-forum_2019_ferrovie-it-trenitalia-sues-delay-app.md), [OR-006](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-006_apple-app-store_2026_flixbus-it.md). Confidence: low.

**Q12. Which legs are planned carefully and which are left to chance?** Open for commuters. The one account found is of a railway enthusiast: he plans the changes and the fallback in detail and leaves the city bus or tram at each end to luck, which is where he loses his connections (F72, F74).

### Comparing and combining options (Q13–Q16)

**Q13. How do commuters choose between modes?** Partial.

- **F12.** The car dominates everywhere measured: 60.8% of trips in Italy, 63% in France, 53% in Germany, 69% of distance in Switzerland, 68.7% of US workers driving alone. Italy has the highest car ownership and the lowest car occupancy of thirteen EU countries; on work trips in Switzerland 92% of car legs carry one person. Sources: [GR-008](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-008_isfort_2025_rapporto-mobilita-22-sintesi.md), [GR-042](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-042_sdes_2023_mobilite-locale-et-longue-distance-des-francais.md), [GR-041](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-041_bmv-infas_2025_mobilitaet-in-deutschland-2023.md), [GR-035](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-035_bfs-are_2023_mikrozensus-mobilitaet-verkehr-2021.md), [GR-016](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-016_eurostat_2021_passenger-mobility-statistics.md). Confidence: high.
- **F13.** Reliability and frequency make public transport attractive, but improving reliability alone moves demand little, because car users already have reliability and flexibility. Convenience comes first when choosing (42% in Sydney, cost 28%, sustainability 7%); public transport is considered only if it takes less than about double the driving time. The car costs little at the point of use because its fixed costs are already paid. 22% of Europeans find it hard to change habits. Sources: [AP-001](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-001_goransson_2023_factors-that-make-public.md) (PDF p.7, p.11), [GR-037](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-037_imove-australia_2021_sydney-maas-trial-final-report.md) (PDF p.76, p.120), [GR-009](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-009_oecd-itf_2021_integrating-public-transport-into-maas.md) (PDF p.13), [GR-006](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-006_european-commission_2026_swd-300-impact-assessment.md) (PDF p.12). Confidence: medium.
- **F52.** Habits change at life events, not through campaigns. Among Europeans who changed habits in five years the reasons are the cost of transport (32%), a change in the family (30%), moving home (21%) and a new job (16%). Free tickets or road closures change behaviour while they last, and "most people return to car use" afterwards. Stated intentions to use the car less usually do not turn into behaviour. Sources: [GR-017](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-017_ipsos-europ-assistance_2025_mobility-barometer.md) (PDF p.19), [AP-017](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-017_vos_2017_travel-satisfaction-revisited-pivotal.md) (PDF p.13), [AP-001](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-001_goransson_2023_factors-that-make-public.md) (PDF p.8), [GR-008](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-008_isfort_2025_rapporto-mobilita-22-sintesi.md) (PDF p.13–14). Confidence: medium.
- **F56.** Habit can be measured, and many commuters do not choose in any real sense. Among 1,615 urban commuters in England, being a "habitual driver" (using a car every week and agreeing that it is done "automatically") multiplies the odds of commuting by train by 0.10, by bus by 0.07 and on foot or by bike by 0.14, with income, distance and the number of vehicles held constant; 83% had a vehicle in the household and just over half were habitual drivers. The walk to the bus stop, the frequency of buses and the walk to the station lose their effect in the full model, while attitudes keep theirs. A review of 36 studies of commuting during the pandemic finds the same from the other side: in Bristol 50% of those who drove before kept driving and 3% of public transport users stayed on it; people who do not use a means judge it more harshly than those who do (nine studies); and those who kept using public transport were mostly those with no alternative, so the authors write of mode "use" where there is no "choice". Sources: [AP-027](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-027_keyes_2018_changing-influences-commuting-mode.md) (table 4, PDF p.7–8), [AP-012](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-012_zarabi_2023_understanding-travel-mode-choice.md) (PDF p.23–24, p.27). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: high (two independent sources agree, one of grade A). Both are urban; the English data are from 2009–2010 and the review covers an exceptional period.
- **F63.** Asked why they used the means they used, people answer "the simplest" far more often than time, cost or habit. In Switzerland, for single legs and as an open question: by car, "the simplest or most comfortable solution" 37.9%, travel time 16.7%, no other possibility 16.2%, carrying things 12.3%, habit 2.1%, cost 1.2%; by public transport, simplest or most comfortable 36.6%, no alternative 19.9%, travel time 8.5%, habit 5.0%, holding a pass 5.0%. In the Sydney trial convenience was the most important criterion for 43%, cost for 28%. Sources: [GR-035](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-035_bfs-are_2023_mikrozensus-mobilitaet-verkehr-2021.md) (table T3.3.1.3, PDF p.34), [GR-037](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-037_imove-australia_2021_sydney-maas-trial-final-report.md) (PDF p.187). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: medium (two grade B sources agree). People hardly ever name habit themselves, while the models find it decisive (F56): the two are not in conflict, because a habit is lived as "the simplest". One in six car legs and one in five public transport legs are described as having no alternative.
- **F64.** Almost every household has a car, and half of the owners say they could use it less. A car is in the household of 87% of respondents in ten European countries and of 96% in Italy, the highest of the ten. Among car owners 55% in Europe and 58% in Italy say they could consider reducing car use in the next six months (14% "certainly"), and 32% could consider no longer owning one (37% in Italy); 68% could not. Users of public transport who expect to use it more outnumber those who expect less by 12 points in Europe and 10 in Italy; for the car the balance is −2. In Germany car sharing, rental bicycles, rental scooters and on-demand services together are under 1% of trips. Sources: [GR-017](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-017_ipsos-europ-assistance_2025_mobility-barometer.md) (PDF p.27, p.32, p.48–49, read from page images), [GR-041](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-041_bmv-infas_2025_mobilitaet-in-deutschland-2023.md) (PDF p.117–127). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: low (stated intentions in an online panel paid for by an insurer; the German figure is one grade B source on a different measure). The sample is the general population, not commuters.
- Still open: the criteria Italian intercity commuters name themselves, and in which order.

**Q14. How do people compare options described in different ways?** Partial.

- **F14.** On 100 EU routes, options combining modes existed on 76%, but platforms rarely show or combine them. In the 2024 Eurobarometer (25,805 interviews) 31% had never booked a journey combining modes; among those who rarely or never do, 17% could not find a suitable combination and 5% could not buy all tickets in one place. The Commission's own estimate of the gain is small: at most 1.7% more rail journeys. Source: [GR-006](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-006_european-commission_2026_swd-300-impact-assessment.md) (PDF p.12–13). Confidence: medium.
- **F15.** The rail industry reads the same survey as 61% finding multimodal booking easy. Source: [AR-003](Sources/Articles/AR-003_cer_2025_eurobarometer-press-release.md). Confidence: low (interested party). The two readings differ in emphasis more than in numbers.
- People do not believe planner results they cannot check: a passenger doubts that a trip with three changes of five minutes is faster than the direct one ([OF-002](Sources/Online_Forums/OF-002_eisenbahnforum_2016_eisenbahnforum-reiseerlebnisse-bahn.md)); two phones give different trains for the same search, and a teenager "has no trust in the app's information" ([OR-013](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-013_apple-app-store_2026_sncf-connect-fr.md)). A German reviewer asks for "a convenient way to compare alternatives over several routes" ([OR-007](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-007_apple-app-store_2026_db-navigator-de.md)). Showing the price next to each alternative is valued ([OR-003](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-003_apple-app-store_2026_citymapper-gb.md)). Confidence: low.

**Q15. Where do connections between legs work or break?** Partial, well covered.

- **F16.** A change costs more than its minutes. Walking and waiting weigh about twice the time on board; the pure cost of a change in Madrid was estimated at 15 to 18 minutes of travel time; a change between bus and rail costs about twice one between trains; a guaranteed connection is worth about 9 minutes. Focus groups in Madrid and Vitoria name two costs not tied to time: the mental effort of staying alert, and the interruption of what one was doing ("I take the longest route with the fewest possible transfers, even though it takes longer"). Real-time information makes the wait feel shorter. Sources: [AP-001](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-001_goransson_2023_factors-that-make-public.md) (PDF p.4, p.6), [AP-021](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-021_jimenez_2018_user-perception-transfers-multimodal.md) (PDF p.8–11), [AP-028](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-028_shires_2018_impact-planned-disruptions-rail.md) (PDF p.18). Leg: transfer. Confidence: high (urban studies; the authors warn that changes between cities may differ).
- **F48.** Planners propose the tightest change and regular travellers do not trust it. A Swiss commuter is always shown the bus that leaves 4 to 5 minutes at the station, "the buses are always late in the morning and I have missed the train several times", and now searches bus and train separately. A Trenitalia reviewer asks for "alternative combinations with wider connections". A US reviewer has the opposite problem: the app assumes a planned connection cannot be made. In German travel diaries a 3-minute connection decides an 8-hour trip, and a delay becomes hours when the last train of the evening is missed. Sources: [OR-014](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-014_apple-app-store_2026_sbb-mobile-ch.md) (2026-08-18), [OR-009](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-009_apple-app-store_2026_trenitalia-app-it.md) (2026-08-08), [OR-004](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-004_apple-app-store_2026_transit-us.md), [OF-002](Sources/Online_Forums/OF-002_eisenbahnforum_2016_eisenbahnforum-reiseerlebnisse-bahn.md). Focus groups in England describe the buffer in their own words: "wiggle room" and a "wait time dilemma" (F9). Leg: transfer, first mile. Confidence: medium for the buffer travellers add (reviews from four countries and one grade A study); low for what planners propose (single reviews).
- **F17.** Operators sell connections they do not keep: "combinations of connections that you cannot keep because of delays and trains that do not wait" (Trenitalia); "impossible to make a connection with another bus or a train" (FlixBus); "missed connections … we have no other choice on many routes" (SNCF). Sources: [OR-009](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-009_apple-app-store_2026_trenitalia-app-it.md), [OR-006](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-006_apple-app-store_2026_flixbus-it.md), [OR-013](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-013_apple-app-store_2026_sncf-connect-fr.md). Confidence: low.

**Q16. What trade-offs do people accept, and how much do first and last mile weigh?** Partial.

- Access to public transport is on foot: in Switzerland 68% of walks to a stop are 500 m or less, and car or bike access is rare. People walk about 150 m to a shared bike and up to 500 m to a train. Sources: [GR-035](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-035_bfs-are_2023_mikrozensus-mobilitaet-verkehr-2021.md) (PDF p.39), [GR-012](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-012_uitp_2023_policy-brief-mobility-hubs.md) (PDF p.3). Confidence: medium.
- Help for the first and last mile has had little effect in trials: a 5-dollar incentive for ride-hailing to the station attracted very few people in Sydney; an automated shuttle to a Danish campus did not raise public transport use; shared mobility hubs in European pilots were known by over 60% and little used, and not for commuting. Sources: [GR-037](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-037_imove-australia_2021_sydney-maas-trial-final-report.md), [AP-001](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-001_goransson_2023_factors-that-make-public.md) (PDF p.7), [GR-015](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-015_eumo_2025_shared-mobility-hubs-sharedimobihub.md). In Orlando taxi and ride-hailing are 0.3 to 0.5% of the trips to and from bus and commuter-rail stops, and nine in ten passengers walk at both ends ([AP-010](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-010_azimi_2020_mode-choice-behavior-access.md), table 2, PDF p.6). Confidence: medium. Operators' own data say the opposite for scooters and bikes ([GR-010](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-010_egum_2024_public-transport-shared-mobility.md)), with an interest.
- A feeder to a trunk line "will not work if that line is unattractive or the trip is less convenient than by car" ([GR-009](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-009_oecd-itf_2021_integrating-public-transport-into-maas.md), PDF p.14).
- The three papers on the legs to and from the station have now been read: see F53 for how long each leg is. Each change in a journey lowers the odds of reaching it by anything but walking ([AP-010](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-010_azimi_2020_mode-choice-behavior-access.md), tables 6 and 7); waiting and changing weigh more than riding among Dhaka commuters too ([AP-008](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-008_rahman_2022_first-last-mile-public.md), PDF p.8); among the Suzhou–Shanghai commuters a trip from home to the station under 30 minutes, and walking or cycling to and from the stations, go with better reported health ([AP-025](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-025_wang_2020_exploring-physical-mental-health.md), PDF p.9, p.11). Confidence: medium (three studies outside Europe; mechanisms only).
- **F74.** The first and last legs are where the chain breaks and where nobody protects the connection. In the same diaries: a city bus to the station that loses 8 minutes in the rain costs a missed train and a new ticket of 71.75 euro; a late train reaches the last bus only because the bus is late too ("protecting a connection in Germany mostly means hoping that the connection is later than the feeder"); after midnight the choice is a run for the last bus, a 20-minute wait or 2 km on foot; relatives' cars are the backup at both ends ("if the bus does not come, call and I will drive you"), and other passengers phone home to be fetched. Replacement buses are the weakest link: unlabelled, unknown to the app and to the displays. With separate tickets "the customer alone carries the risk of the way". In Japanese suburban and mountain areas residents say the same of local services: "the connection between public transport services is not smooth, so I use the car". Sources: [OF-002](Sources/Online_Forums/OF-002_eisenbahnforum_2016_eisenbahnforum-reiseerlebnisse-bahn.md) (posts of 2 August 2015, 19–20 September 2017, 3 January 2020, 31 May 2021, 14 November 2021, 8 July 2022), [GR-046](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-046_digital-agency-japan_2025_idou-juyou-no-haaku-saishu-houkokusho.md) (workshops, PDF p.129, p.135; working translation from Japanese). Leg: first and last mile, transfer. Confidence: low (a grade C thread and one grade B study from a different context). It fits F50 and F69: these legs are also outside the rules on tickets and rights.

### Information during the journey (Q17–Q19)

**Q17. What do commuters need to know on each leg, and when?** Partial.

- At a change: "when I must be on which platform", not a map; on board: where the trip stands and whether the change still holds; at the stop: whether to wait, switch or give up. Information during the trip is rated more important than before it (83% against 65% among rail passengers in an older US survey), and it has value even when nothing can be changed, because it lets people warn others and lowers anxiety. Sources: [OR-014](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-014_apple-app-store_2026_sbb-mobile-ch.md), [OR-015](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-015_apple-app-store_2026_9292-nl.md) (2025-11-23), [GR-040](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-040_trb_2003_tcrp-92-traveler-information.md) (PDF p.18, p.27–29). Leg: transfer, main leg. Confidence: medium.
- What prospective users ask a combined app to tell them, in English focus groups: first that the information is live and right; then what a missed connection implies ("is there another service in 10 min, 1 h..."), where the vehicle is, the total time and cost against the car with its parking, whether the station has parking and staff, and how reliable a service has been. Least useful: health and environmental effects, adverts. Source: [AP-016](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-016_mcilroy_2024_reservation-i-have-that.md) (PDF p.8–10, tables A1 and A2). Confidence: medium (one grade A study, qualitative).
- Crowding is a kind of live information that commuters mostly do not have. In a stated-choice survey of 377 people at tram and bus stops in Krakow, 45 to 75% would let a severely overcrowded vehicle go and wait 5 to 10 minutes for the next. Among those who must arrive on time 43% would board anyway, against 15% of the others, and people who use the line five days a week wait less than occasional users. Source: [AP-011](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-011_drabicki_2023_should-i-stay-or.md) (table 3, PDF p.6–7). Leg: main leg, urban. Confidence: medium (one grade A study; stated, not observed; short trips with a vehicle every 5 to 10 minutes, which is not the intercity case).
- Still open: the same for the first and last mile, and for the car.

**Q18. Where is information fragmented, late or inconsistent?** Partial.

- **F18.** Information depends on who owns the data. In 2019 Trenitalia sued a third-party app that showed delays on more than 3 million phones; a forum user wrote that he is "spoilt for choice to know where a plane is" and needs "a crystal ball" for a train. The rest of the thread, read later, adds that the operator said the app made about 800,000 requests a day to its sales system, that users found the third-party app "much more usable" than the operator's, that one proposed a public interface offered by the infrastructure manager as for flights, and that the court of Rome did not suspend the app in the interim proceedings. The planner 9292 answers a complaint by saying it depends on what operators send. In Japan one railway group's app cannot search with real-time delays or sell another group's tickets. Sources: [OF-001](Sources/Online_Forums/OF-001_ferrovie-it-forum_2019_ferrovie-it-trenitalia-sues-delay-app.md), [OR-015](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-015_apple-app-store_2026_9292-nl.md) (2026-09-17), [GR-047](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-047_mlit_2025_chiiki-koutsuu-digital-gijutsu-report.md) (PDF p.13–16), [GR-055](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-055_gtfs-de_nd_realtime-data-germany.md). Confidence: medium.
- **F19.** Live status is what regular rail travellers rely on and it often fails: Italo's tracking "almost never works" for a Turin–Milan carnet holder; Trenitalia's "is always wrong" on one line; in Germany a daily user acts "as if the train came on time" whatever the app says. Two apps are needed for one trip when the bus delay disappears from the rail app. Sources: [OR-010](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-010_apple-app-store_2026_italo-it.md), [OR-009](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-009_apple-app-store_2026_trenitalia-app-it.md), [OR-007](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-007_apple-app-store_2026_db-navigator-de.md). Leg: main leg, transfer. Confidence: medium (more than ten independent reviews: a perception).
- **F69.** On the car leg, and on the bus, the traveller has less information and fewer rights than on the train. The Italian regulator set the first rules on motorway users' rights in 2024: a right to information for "planning the trip" and for "managing unforeseen events during the route", and, because the network is run by different concessionaires, a duty to inform together "so as not to leave the user the burden of knowing which concessionaire to consult"; a single national app is foreseen. For bus transport "no compensation for delay is provided"; of 45,317 conciliation requests from April 2023 to June 2025, 97.3% concerned air travel, 1.5% rail and about 0.4% bus. Of 54 bus stations under the rules, 33 had published the prospectus that must say how the public is informed, and an inspection of the Roma Tiburtina bus station found shortcomings in it. Source: [GR-003](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-003_art_2025_relazione-annuale.md) (PDF p.83–87, p.132, p.138–140; working translation from Italian). Leg: main leg by car or coach, transfer. Confidence: low (one grade B source). Few requests do not mean few problems: for a bus delay there is nothing to claim.

**Q19. Which sources do people trust?** Weak.

- 45% of British rail passengers have low or no trust in the rail industry, yet most would recommend the train. A French regular asks the operator not to announce a delay "if you are not at least somewhat sure" of its length. Bad underlying data destroys trust in the tool that shows it: "ghost buses", times that drivers themselves call wrong. Sources: [GR-051](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-051_transport-focus_2026_rail-customer-experience-survey.md) (section 11), [OR-013](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-013_apple-app-store_2026_sncf-connect-fr.md), [OF-003](Sources/Online_Forums/OF-003_hacker-news_2023_hn-citymapper-joins-via.md), [OR-004](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-004_apple-app-store_2026_transit-us.md), [GR-040](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-040_trb_2003_tcrp-92-traveler-information.md) (PDF p.110). Confidence: low.
- Trust in a new tool depends on its being right, and experienced travellers trust themselves first. In English focus groups: "One bad experience (i.e., not real time info leading to missing a connection ...) will stop people using it"; "I absolutely do not trust app services which are not run directly by the company I am trying to buy the service from". In interviews in three English cities: "I can probably work out all my alternative routes quicker in my head than this app can". Older research finds that people keep "favoured, familiar" sources and a back-up. Sources: [AP-016](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-016_mcilroy_2024_reservation-i-have-that.md) (table A1, PDF p.15, p.17), [AP-004](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-004_alyavina_2020_mobility-as-service-sustainable.md) (PDF p.10), [AP-003](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-003_lyons_2019_importance-user-perspective-evolution.md) (PDF p.11). Confidence: high that this is what people say (two independent grade A studies agree); both asked about a service described to them, not one they had used.
- Which source a commuter believes when two disagree is still open. One experienced traveller's answer is: none of them fully, and his own experience first (F73).
- **F73.** The same traveller checks several channels, finds that they contradict each other, and trusts his own reading most. Before leaving he gets a delay alert by e-mail and re-checks during the day; on the way he compares the app, the platform display, the announcements and the staff. He records a display showing +40 while the app shows the train on time, two conductors giving opposite instructions, a replacement train that the app does not know, another operator's staff saying "we do not use the same apps and information channels", and forecasts he does not believe ("Too often the delay was much smaller or not there at all"; a forecast of +45 that becomes +25 on arrival). Staff often know no more: "we do not quite know ourselves where we are going and when". Late information removes the alternative: a fault is announced after the train he could have taken has left. Source: [OF-002](Sources/Online_Forums/OF-002_eisenbahnforum_2016_eisenbahnforum-reiseerlebnisse-bahn.md) (posts of 10 July 2015, 4 November 2015, 23 December 2016, 24 June 2017, 15 October 2018, 14 November 2021, 3 November 2022, 27 December 2024). Leg: main leg, transfer. Confidence: low (one thread, grade C). It is one person's account of what many reviews report (F43); it does not say which channel an ordinary commuter believes.

### When plans change (Q20–Q23)

**Q20. Which unexpected situations occur, on which leg?** Partial for rail, open for the rest.

- 17% of British rail passengers said their journey was delayed, 62% of them by less than ten minutes. On an average weekday in Great Britain there are about 39 planned engineering disruptions, most served by replacement bus. In Italy 229 extreme weather events interrupted rail services between 2010 and 2025. In Switzerland 21% of drivers are in a traffic jam on the way to work at least once a week. Sources: [GR-051](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-051_transport-focus_2026_rail-customer-experience-survey.md), [AP-028](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-028_shires_2018_impact-planned-disruptions-rail.md) (table 1), [GR-004](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-004_legambiente_2025_pendolaria-20-edizione.md) (PDF p.24), [GR-035](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-035_bfs-are_2023_mikrozensus-mobilitaet-verkehr-2021.md) (PDF p.37). Confidence: medium. Nothing is broken down by leg, and nothing covers parking or the last mile.

**Q21. How do people find out, and how early?** Partial.

- **F43.** Delay information is late or wrong in both directions. Too late: the delay announced in the minute the train should leave, the platform change two minutes before (Germany); a bus shown on time for three hours and then cancelled (Italy). Wrong the other way: a 40-minute delay announced and then the bus on time; the train in the station before the delay shown has passed. Stale: a notification to go to the platform for a train cancelled six hours earlier; "all green" on the commuter screen while stuck in the train. Too many: 8 to 10 identical notices every morning, so the user turns them off and loses the useful ones. Sources: [OR-007](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-007_apple-app-store_2026_db-navigator-de.md), [OR-006](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-006_apple-app-store_2026_flixbus-it.md), [OR-013](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-013_apple-app-store_2026_sncf-connect-fr.md), [OR-014](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-014_apple-app-store_2026_sbb-mobile-ch.md), [OR-009](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-009_apple-app-store_2026_trenitalia-app-it.md). Leg: main leg. Confidence: medium (many independent reviews in five countries: a perception).
- **F68.** Telling a regular traveller about a problem on a saved journey before leaving is an idea of the early 2000s that reached few people. A US review of 2003 describes a pilot in Magdeburg that notifies a user "when there is a delay or a disruption to their selected journey" and a project in Karlsruhe with a "Delay Manager" that "independently checks previously planned journeys" before and during the trip. On a commuter railway into Washington 6,500 passengers had registered for e-mail alerts against 12,000 to 14,000 one-way trips a day, and everyone received the same message whatever their line. In 2001 only 4.7% of 637 US transit websites offered an alert sign-up and 1.9% real-time information. One agency found that "having to register to use the service discourages people". Operators bought vehicle location for operations, not for traveller information. Source: [GR-040](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-040_trb_2003_tcrp-92-traveler-information.md) (PDF p.42–43, p.67, p.76–82). Leg: main leg. Confidence: low (one grade C source, old). The need described in F45 was already recognised twenty years ago; what the sources do not say is why it is still reported as unmet in the reviews (F43).
- Knowing about a planned disruption before reaching the station changes behaviour little ([AP-028](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-028_shires_2018_impact-planned-disruptions-rail.md), PDF p.28; non-commuters). In Italy an operator was fined twice for not notifying cancellations and delays to passengers who had left their contact details ([GR-003](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-003_art_2025_relazione-annuale.md), PDF p.143).
- Two papers give a frame for this question. Passengers change what they do when the delay is long, about 30 minutes or more ([AP-023](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-023_mo_2022_inferring-passenger-responses-urban.md), PDF p.6, citing an earlier study). Information in a disruption can be described by who is reached, where, when and with what; the least every passenger gets is to find out "at the time they try to board a service which is not running anymore" ([AP-009](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-009_leng_2020_role-information-availability-passengers.md), PDF p.7–8).

**Q22. What do they do next?** Partial.

- **F20.** Among delayed rail passengers in Great Britain, 60% were satisfied with how easy it was to find information, but only 37% with the information on alternative routes, onward travel or connections, and 40% with how clearly the reason was explained. Focus groups say the same: "customers would like timely and honest explanations of delays"; they forgive the fault more easily than the lack of information. Sources: [GR-051](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-051_transport-focus_2026_rail-customer-experience-survey.md) (section 4), [GR-040](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-040_trb_2003_tcrp-92-traveler-information.md) (PDF p.18), [GR-027](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-027_orr_2026_annual-rail-consumer-report-2025-26.md). An interviewee in London describes the result: "with the on-route issues often the information that comes through is poor ... And then you almost end up making that decision yourself" ([AP-004](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-004_alyavina_2020_mobility-as-service-sustainable.md), PDF p.10). Leg: main leg, transfer. Confidence: high.
- **F42.** Apps do not carry the traveller through a disruption. "My train was late and as a result I missed the connection but no suggestion by the app for alternatives" (Trainline). Alternatives "are often not feasible as train delays and cancellations are not taken into account"; after tapping an alternative "one no longer has any idea of the current status of the original trip"; after a missed connection the app congratulates on the punctual arrival of the missed train (DB Navigator). One ticket exchanged and all the tickets of a trip with connections are deleted (SNCF). The operator's own page admits that an alternative updates the plan, not the ticket. At a station in Germany staff were "as lost as the passengers", and a late announcement removed the one alternative: "why did he not say so earlier, I could have gone via Gera". Sources: [OR-005](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-005_apple-app-store_2026_trainline-gb.md), [OR-007](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-007_apple-app-store_2026_db-navigator-de.md), [OR-013](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-013_apple-app-store_2026_sncf-connect-fr.md), [PR-007](Sources/Products/PR-007_db-fernverkehr_2026_db-navigator.md), [OF-002](Sources/Online_Forums/OF-002_eisenbahnforum_2016_eisenbahnforum-reiseerlebnisse-bahn.md). Confidence: medium.
- **F21.** What people actually do. Faced with planned works, 66 to 74% took the replacement bus and about 20% left rail; with a diverted train about 9% left. They treat the disruption as a fixed cost more than counting minutes, tolerate a single day and resent a week. Over the following year 10 to 13% of rail trips were lost. In reviews, people buy a second ticket, take a taxi (63, 600 euros) or go back to their first plan. Sources: [AP-028](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-028_shires_2018_impact-planned-disruptions-rail.md) (PDF p.15, p.26–28), [OR-006](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-006_apple-app-store_2026_flixbus-it.md), [OR-015](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-015_apple-app-store_2026_9292-nl.md), [OR-011](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-011_apple-app-store_2026_kakao-t-kr.md). Confidence: medium. The study excluded commuters on purpose. For unplanned disruptions see F57.
- **F57.** In an unplanned stoppage most passengers reroute if another line is near, and what they know decides how much time they lose. Observed: when two trains collided on the Chicago network at 9:09 on 24 September 2019 and five stations were blocked for 70 minutes, fare-card data show that about 70% of the passengers affected stayed on rail by another route, 16% took a bus, 7% waited or left later and 8% left public transport; about 1% or fewer cancelled the trip. A third line ran next to the two blocked ones, and the authors call it a case of "high service redundancy". Passengers who had not yet entered the system rerouted more easily than those put off a train. Modelled: a simulation of a three-hour rail blockage in the Zurich evening peak gives an average delay of 2.4 hours when passengers know nothing and wait, 9.8 minutes when everyone is told at the start what is cancelled and until when, and 1.6 minutes when it is known in advance; with no information 15.6% do not complete their day. Sources: [AP-023](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-023_mo_2022_inferring-passenger-responses-urban.md) (table 2, PDF p.26–29), [AP-009](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-009_leng_2020_role-information-availability-passengers.md) (PDF p.19; table 2, p.22). Leg: main leg, transfer. Confidence: medium for the size of what is at stake (one grade A source, a simulation whose result follows from the behaviour it assumes); low for the shares observed in Chicago (one grade B source, one incident). On a line between cities there is usually no parallel line, so the Chicago shares are a best case. The appendix of the Chicago paper, read later, shows that the split of passengers who did not appear that day between another means and a cancelled trip rests on an assumed share, not on data.
- People put up with it. In focus groups commuting "horror stories" came with the belief that "nothing much can be done about them" ([GR-040](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-040_trb_2003_tcrp-92-traveler-information.md)); a German survey calls transport "shaped by routines".

**Q23. What does a disrupted journey cost?** Partial.

- Satisfaction with the journey falls from 92% with no delay to 69% for 5 to 14 minutes and 40% for 30 minutes or more. A stressful commute may lower performance and satisfaction at work. Three in ten people in five Italian cities gave up work, study or care opportunities because of travel difficulties. Compensation exists and is hard to get: 8.3 million claims a year in Great Britain; in Italy season-ticket holders of two operators were not being paid; reviewers in three countries ask for it to be automatic. Sources: [GR-051](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-051_transport-focus_2026_rail-customer-experience-survey.md), [AP-017](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-017_vos_2017_travel-satisfaction-revisited-pivotal.md) (PDF p.5), [GR-004](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-004_legambiente_2025_pendolaria-20-edizione.md) (PDF p.50), [GR-053](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-053_orr_2025_complaints-delay-compensation-2024-25.md), [GR-003](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-003_art_2025_relazione-annuale.md) (PDF p.142). Confidence: medium.
- **F75.** After a bad trip, what passengers say is that next time they will drive; the regular traveller stays because he has no alternative. Overheard in the diaries: "next time I will certainly drive again"; "by car I would have been much faster and it is cheaper too"; "if we could get off now and take a car we would be there in an hour and a half". A weekend commuter by rail writes that after more than seven years he has "had enough" and is ending it. The main writer says he has no alternative to the train, and sums up: "in Germany success is above all a matter of luck … there is always something", and yet the trains are full. A woman who drives to the station explains that since she starts work later she finds no parking space there and may go back to the earlier train. Source: [OF-002](Sources/Online_Forums/OF-002_eisenbahnforum_2016_eisenbahnforum-reiseerlebnisse-bahn.md) (posts of August 2014, 10 July 2015, 5 June 2019, 1 January 2026). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: low (overheard remarks in one thread, grade C).

### Current tools and workarounds (Q24–Q26)

**Q24. Which tools do commuters use?** Partial.

- **F22.** The 17 products fall into four kinds: general planners, ticket sellers, operators' apps and broader mobility apps. Operators' apps now claim the whole trip: DB Navigator, NS, SNCF Connect and SBB Mobile plan and sell urban transport, show shared bikes and name commuters; SBB claims to accompany the traveller "from door to door". The Italian apps claim less: Trenitalia's page mentions the daily trip once, Italo's speaks to tourists, and Telepass joins car, parking, trains and urban tickets only as payments. Sources: [PR-001](Sources/Products/PR-001_google_2026_google-maps.md) to [PR-017](Sources/Products/PR-017_bodlaender_2026_sprinter.md). Confidence: low (vendors' own pages, nothing tested).
- What people use in fact: among British rail passengers who looked for information, 68% used an app and 18% a general map; 63% of app ticket buyers used the reseller and 20% the operator. In Sydney the preferred tools were the general map and a simple timetable app that saves regular trips; the integrated app came third. In English focus groups the benchmark a new app must beat is "a combination of Google (for route planning) and transport providers' own websites/apps". Sources: [AP-016](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-016_mcilroy_2024_reservation-i-have-that.md) (PDF p.7), [GR-051](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-051_transport-focus_2026_rail-customer-experience-survey.md), [GR-037](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-037_imove-australia_2021_sydney-maas-trial-final-report.md) (PDF p.122). Confidence: medium.
- Still open: which tools an Italian intercity commuter uses on each leg.

**Q25. How do people move between tools?** Weak. A Sydney participant: "App swapping is annoying … checking Google Maps then making sure I have my Opal card then … ordering an Uber because I became late through so many apps" ([AP-014](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-014_smith_2023_mobility-as-service-users.md)). Reviewers check a second app or website when the first fails: the stations' websites for Italo, a regional app for SBB, another seller for Trenitalia. In Italy "every municipality has its own parking app" ([OR-012](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-012_apple-app-store_2026_telepass-it.md)). The Korean ministry describes the normal case as checking the route and then booking each means in its own app ([GR-049](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-049_molit-korea_2023_k-maas-pilot-announcement.md)). In England: "Trying to find out the local bus operator, you might need to find their app, or use Google, so that takes time" ([AP-004](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-004_alyavina_2020_mobility-as-service-sustainable.md), PDF p.12); "I find it rather complicated navigating multiple websites for each type of transport to find my journey" ([AP-015](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-015_mcilroy_2023_this-where-public-transport.md), PDF p.9). Confidence: low.

**Q26. What workarounds have people developed?** Weak, no longer open. Taking an earlier train or bus than the planner proposes; searching each leg separately; acting as if the train were on time whatever the delay shown; choosing the route with fewest changes; keeping a ticket on paper; splitting tickets by hand; following a companion who knows the station; combining one service with a leg under one's own control and avoiding two services in a row (F9). Sources: [AP-015](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-015_mcilroy_2023_this-where-public-transport.md) (PDF p.11), [GR-004](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-004_legambiente_2025_pendolaria-20-edizione.md), [OR-014](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-014_apple-app-store_2026_sbb-mobile-ch.md), [OR-007](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-007_apple-app-store_2026_db-navigator-de.md), [AP-021](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-021_jimenez_2018_user-perception-transfers-multimodal.md), [OR-005](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-005_apple-app-store_2026_trainline-gb.md). Confidence: low. Italian commuter groups, where most of this would be found, could not be read.

- **F72.** An experienced traveller manages connections with rules and records of his own. Over twelve years of diaries on a German railway forum, one regular traveller logs every trip (568 by 2019) and keeps his own counts: 77 of 80 transfers of 3 to 7 minutes made at his usual junction; 80.6% of connections made in the first half of 2020, "by far the lowest value" since 2014; a bad trip about every tenth to fifteenth. The rules he and readers state: not the last connection of the day; a charged phone and a power bank; "anything under 10 minutes of transfer time is no connection for me"; book through journeys or build in a buffer; know the fallback route; stand at the door nearest the stairs; ask the conductor to phone ahead so that the next train waits; do not tell anyone you will be late "before the connection has really gone". Source: [OF-002](Sources/Online_Forums/OF-002_eisenbahnforum_2016_eisenbahnforum-reiseerlebnisse-bahn.md) (posts of 6 January 2015, 26 September 2016, 19 September 2017, 3 September 2020, 8 July 2022; German, working translation). Leg: transfer. Confidence: low (one thread, grade C, mostly one writer who travels long distances at weekends and is not a daily commuter). It agrees with the "wiggle room" of F9 and the distrust of tight changes in F48, and shows what the interviews should look for: each commuter's own rules and how they were learned.

### Why now (Q27–Q33)

**Q27. Why do the frictions persist?** Partial, see F25 to F31, F49, F50 and F58 to F61.

**Q28. What do current tools do well, and where do they stop?** Partial.

- **F23.** In 1,555 recent App Store reviews of the 17 apps, 53% gave one or two stars. A keyword coding of those found tickets, payment and refunds first (27%), then bugs (15%), real-time information (11%), prices (11%) and a recent redesign (10%). The reviews have now all been read, and the reading by theme for each app is in the source files. It confirms the order and adds what the keywords missed: most anger follows an update; apps are judged for the service behind them (F47); regular users want less on the screen (F45). Sources: [OR-001](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-001_apple-app-store_2026_google-maps-it.md) to [OR-017](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-017_apple-app-store_2026_sprinter-nl.md). Confidence: low. Recent written reviews are far more negative than the whole user base.
- **F24.** What tools do well: buying a simple ticket; making an unknown city usable; showing the vehicle approaching. Where they stop: at the change, in a disruption, at the edge of the operator's own network, and at the door ("we'll get you to the door" gave a three-hour walk). Sources: [OR-003](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-003_apple-app-store_2026_citymapper-gb.md), [OR-005](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-005_apple-app-store_2026_trainline-gb.md), [OF-003](Sources/Online_Forums/OF-003_hacker-news_2023_hn-citymapper-joins-via.md). Confidence: low.
- **F47.** Apps are blamed for the service. "If the app … does not tell me the constant delays of the regional trains, the traveller can no longer rely even on the app" (Moovit, Milan); "the app is quick but the trains are not" (NS); "in 7 days of commuting, maybe twice they are on time" (Trenitalia). The reverse also holds for Italo: "how can such a good service have such an embarrassing app". Sources: [OR-002](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-002_apple-app-store_2026_moovit-it.md), [OR-008](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-008_apple-app-store_2026_ns-app-nl.md), [OR-009](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-009_apple-app-store_2026_trenitalia-app-it.md), [OR-010](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-010_apple-app-store_2026_italo-it.md), [OR-004](Sources/Online_Reviews/OR-004_apple-app-store_2026_transit-us.md). Confidence: medium.
- **F60.** No planner covers the whole job. An expert scoring of 14 European journey planners in 2019 against ten aspects gave the best one, the German railways' planner, 63 points of 100, and 44 on average. City and regional planners scored well on route planning and live data and poorly on tickets (Budapest's planner: 10 of 10 on live data, 6 of 20 on booking and payment); operators' planners the reverse (German railways: 16 of 20 on booking and payment, 6 of 10 on live data); planners that span countries had neither (Google Maps: 0 of 10 on live data and 4 of 20 on booking and payment; Rome2Rio: 0 and 1). Seven of the 14 combined modes in one trip, most of them two modes only. Source: [AP-026](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-026_esztergarkiss_2019_framework-aspects-evaluation-multimodal.md) (table 4, PDF p.8–10). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: low (one grade B study: scores given by unnamed experts on a list of features, not by users on a journey; websites as they were in 2019).
- Commercial content gets in the way at the urgent moment: a compulsory advert that made a user miss a bus (Moovit); insurance and car hire offers that hide the trip (SNCF); marketing notices that push users to switch all notifications off (Trainline, Trenitalia). Confidence: medium (many reviews).

**Q29. Missing information, or information that exists but is scattered or untrusted?** Partial. The information on the normal trip exists and is easy to find (95% of British passengers found planning information easily). What is missing is specific: what to do next in a disruption (F20, F42), a delay estimate one can act on (F43), and one view across operators (F18). Trust is damaged by wrong data more than by scattered data.

- **F61.** Researchers rank the needs in the same order: services that work together first, then information in one place, then payment in one place. A paper on the user's view of combined mobility proposes this as a "hierarchy of user need": "Fundamental in this hierarchy is the existence of viable travel alternatives to the private car. Only then do the higher levels concerning information for journey planning and booking and payment facilities come into their own". From older research on travel information it adds that familiar and predictable trips create little demand for information, that demand peaks at disruption, and that information follows behaviour: "travel behaviour having a stronger impact on information use than vice versa". The aim is not new: the United Kingdom ran a national door-to-door planner for car and public transport from 2004 to 2014. Source: [AP-003](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-003_lyons_2019_importance-user-perspective-evolution.md) (PDF p.7, p.10–11, p.13). Confidence: low (one grade B source: a proposal that was not tested on users, drawing on research mostly older than 2015). It is consistent with F31, F44 and F45.

**Q30. What prevents integration between services?** Partial, well covered.

- **F25.** Since 2017 the EU requires a national access point for travel data; real-time data were optional until the 2024 amendment. Italy's access point collects static data from 16 regions, Trenitalia, Italo and FlixBus; four regions are missing. In May 2026 the Commission proposed rules on rail ticketing and multimodal booking. Sources: [GR-021](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-021_european-union_2017_delegated-regulation-2017-1926.md), [GR-054](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-054_cciss_nd_italian-national-access-point-mmtis.md), [GR-006](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-006_european-commission_2026_swd-300-impact-assessment.md), [AR-001](Sources/Articles/AR-001_railway-gazette_2026_passenger-package.md). Confidence: high.
- **F50.** The new rules stop at the city limits. The proposed regulation does not apply to services whose main purpose is to serve an urban centre or to connect it with the surrounding area, so the first and last legs of a commute stay outside the single ticket and its rights. Planners that inform without selling, and operators' own apps, are also outside. Two tickets for one journey mean no rights when a connection is missed. In Italy fare systems do not even carry over between train categories: "if a high-speed train is late a traveller cannot choose to board an Intercity or a regional train". Sources: [GR-007](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-007_european-commission_2026_com-231-multimodal-booking.md) (Article 2, PDF p.15), [GR-006](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-006_european-commission_2026_swd-300-impact-assessment.md) (PDF p.6, p.14), [GR-004](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-004_legambiente_2025_pendolaria-20-edizione.md). Prospective users ask the same of a combined app: if a train is missed, "who would you then go to for compensation" ([AP-016](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-016_mcilroy_2024_reservation-i-have-that.md), table A1, PDF p.17). Leg: first and last mile, transfer. Confidence: high.
- **F26.** Commercial interest is a barrier. Incumbents' own channels hold about 80 to 90% of online long-distance rail sales in Germany, France and Spain; a German court forbade the operator's app from presetting a filter that favoured its own trains; operators fear losing the customer to a "gatekeeper" and are "reluctant to cede 'ownership' of their customer" ([AP-003](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-003_lyons_2019_importance-user-perspective-evolution.md), PDF p.2); the railways' association calls the 2026 proposals "unjustified regulatory interventionism". Sources: [GR-006](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-006_european-commission_2026_swd-300-impact-assessment.md) (PDF p.16, p.18), [GR-009](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-009_oecd-itf_2021_integrating-public-transport-into-maas.md) (PDF p.27), [AR-001](Sources/Articles/AR-001_railway-gazette_2026_passenger-package.md). Confidence: high.
- **F70.** Integration also fails on the number of operators and on equipment that does not talk to each other. In Italy local public transport by road is run by 280 urban firms and 417 suburban and interurban firms, most of them small (201 and 286 under one million bus-kilometres a year), with 173 contract lots in Lazio, 83 in Sicily and 67 in Lombardy; regional rail has 41 contracts; the regulator writes of a "structural lack of data" available to the public bodies and of "frequent difficulty in obtaining service data from the incumbent". In Japan 60% of 168 services labelled MaaS include one mode only, a quarter of those with several operators share no function at all, and one ticket across operators is rare because each vendor's validation interface is proprietary; timetable data and smart-card data write stops differently and cannot be matched. In Sydney the app developer connected the public transport card through "hacks or work arounds" that broke when the provider changed its site. Sources: [GR-003](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-003_art_2025_relazione-annuale.md) (PDF p.90–95, p.107–108, p.116–117), [GR-047](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-047_mlit_2025_chiiki-koutsuu-digital-gijutsu-report.md) (PDF p.46–49, p.59–61), [GR-037](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-037_imove-australia_2021_sydney-maas-trial-final-report.md) (PDF p.112–113, p.210). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: medium (three grade B sources agree). An interurban commute in Italy crosses several of these firms by construction.
- **F71.** The Commission's own assessment expects little change for travellers and leaves the first and last leg out. In its survey the first reason for never booking a journey that combines modes is "It was not needed" (29%), then the journey would take too long (22%), no suitable combination (14%), the risk of being stranded (12%); only 4% say they could not buy all tickets in one place. 76% book online and 26% compare. The effort of searching is described and not quantified. Measures for the walking and cycling legs were discarded as out of scope, the route tests go from city centre to city centre, and the benefit-to-cost ratio is 0.9 without the external savings. The Regulatory Scrutiny Board gave a negative opinion in 2023 and a positive one with reservations in 2026, writing that the market failures were "not sufficiently demonstrated". On habits the report itself says: "Even if the use of AI tools would help … habits are not easily changed". Under both proposals platforms and operators can complain to an enforcement body; passengers are given no complaint of their own. Sources: [GR-006](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-006_european-commission_2026_swd-300-impact-assessment.md) (PDF p.51–53, p.66–68, p.85–89, p.127–128, p.227), [GR-007](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-007_european-commission_2026_com-231-multimodal-booking.md) (Article 14, PDF p.26), [GR-020](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-020_european-commission_2026_com-232-rail-ticketing.md) (Articles 9 to 11, PDF p.22–24). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: medium (one grade A source for the assessment; the proposals are the legal texts). It adds to F14 and F50.
- Where it works, one body owns the data or the fare: the Dutch planner belongs to the operators and an independent developer can build on the open data; in Switzerland one pass covers the country; Norway has one trip-planning engine for the whole country; Finland obliged operators by law to open ticketing. Sources: [PR-015](Sources/Products/PR-015_reisinformatiegroep_2026_9292.md), [PR-017](Sources/Products/PR-017_bodlaender_2026_sprinter.md), [PR-014](Sources/Products/PR-014_sbb_2026_sbb-mobile.md), [OF-004](Sources/Online_Forums/OF-004_hacker-news_2025_hn-i-built-my-own-citymapper.md), [GR-039](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-039_nordic-policy-centre_2022_finland-maas-legislation.md). Confidence: medium. Even there the complaints about tight changes and late feeder buses remain (F48).

**Q31. How well do tools reflect personal preferences and context?** Weak. Passes and discount cards are not kept or not counted, so people pay for what they already hold (SNCF, SBB, NS, Trainline). Users object when the app decides for them: changing the route without asking (Google Maps), assigning a taxi 20 minutes away (Kakao T, with the proposal "ask first"). A planner "will tend to give you the quickest time" when the traveller would take a cheaper way that is a minute longer ([AP-004](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-004_alyavina_2020_mobility-as-service-sustainable.md), PDF p.10), which fits what the Lyon experiment measured (F5). Confidence: low.

**Q32. What has changed recently?** Partial, the best covered question. Hybrid work and its link with distance (F3, F38 to F41); the EU package of May 2026 (F25, F50); the Italian MaaS programme (F49); flexible passes; Germany's flat monthly ticket, now the usual ticket of 58% of daily public transport users ([GR-041](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-041_bmv-infas_2025_mobilitaet-in-deutschland-2023.md), p.106–109); a new commuting matrix for Italy, 2021, published in October 2025 and not yet opened ([GR-026](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-026_istat_2025_matrici-contiguita-distanza-pendolarismo.md)).

**Q33. What can be learned from integrated services so far?** Partial, well covered.

- **F27.** In Japan about 43% of MaaS projects from 2019 to 2024 ended as trials; the ministry says one-stop access "has not been achieved" because each railway group builds its own app. Source: [GR-047](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-047_mlit_2025_chiiki-koutsuu-digital-gijutsu-report.md) (PDF p.13–17). Confidence: medium.
- **F28.** MaaS Global (Whim) raised over 149 million euros, had about 10,000 monthly users in Helsinki at its peak and went bankrupt in 2024. Citymapper, with tens of millions of users, was sold at a loss: "consumer-facing routing is a commodity". A Dutch institute sums up the first decade as "the perceived problem was a tech issue, and the solution was an app". Sources: [AR-006](Sources/Articles/AR-006_frost-sullivan_2024_maas-global-collapse.md), [AR-005](Sources/Articles/AR-005_techcrunch_2023_via-acquires-citymapper.md), [OF-003](Sources/Online_Forums/OF-003_hacker-news_2023_hn-citymapper-joins-via.md), [AR-010](Sources/Articles/AR-010_global-fleet_2024_maas-1-is-dead.md). Confidence: medium.
- **F29.** The Sydney trial: 99% of trips without the app; the planner hardly used, "partly due to habit"; what participants valued was the personal onboarding, the single monthly bill ("Do I really spend this much on my travel?") and the discounts. "Much more than an app and a set of subscription plans is needed." Sources: [GR-037](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-037_imove-australia_2021_sydney-maas-trial-final-report.md), [AP-014](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-014_smith_2023_mobility-as-service-users.md). Confidence: medium (one trial, two reports).
- **F30.** A round table of public authorities and operators: the effect of MaaS on public transport demand "remains to be demonstrated at scale"; some argued that "international and intercity seamlessness is of little importance among the user base"; pilots should last at least six months, because people do not rearrange their lives around an offer they expect to end. Source: [GR-009](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-009_oecd-itf_2021_integrating-public-transport-into-maas.md) (PDF p.6, p.14, p.16). Confidence: medium. The Swedish trial where 42% reported a change of behaviour has now been read: see F59.
- **F49.** Italy's national programme cost 56.9 million euros and by May 2026 counted almost 950,000 trips, over 16 per user, which implies about 59,000 users (my calculation). ISFORT says the response of demand to digital innovation "is still weak". Sources: [GR-050](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-050_mit_2026_maas-for-italy-white-paper.md), [GR-008](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-008_isfort_2025_rapporto-mobilita-22-sintesi.md) (PDF p.48). Confidence: medium. The white paper itself was not downloaded.
- **F58.** The evidence in favour of combined mobility services is about intentions, in cities. A systematic review of 29 studies published up to 2022 found that 27 rest on choices among hypothetical packages and two on observed use; commuters were the target in 2 of 14 package designs and trains were included in 4. Current travel habits predict interest better than age or income, and people satisfied with what they use see no need. Results contradict each other on what an integrated app is worth: 104 to 127 francs in a Zurich study, nothing in Tyneside, where "participants value the convenience of MaaS apps, they are not (yet) prepared to pay for it". In 40 interviews in three English cities the authors conclude that ease of access "is not something transport users are willing to pay for as it only allows them to easily access services that do not work up to the required standards", and several interviewees said they could do it themselves with free tools. Almost every package is valid in one city; a review of package design notes that validity in several cities "adds value for long-distance commuters". Sources: [AP-005](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-005_kriswardhana_2022_systematic-literature-review-mobility.md) (PDF p.3, p.5–9), [AP-007](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-007_reck_2020_maas-bundle-design.md) (PDF p.5, p.8), [AP-004](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-004_alyavina_2020_mobility-as-service-sustainable.md) (PDF p.13, p.16–17). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: high (three independent grade A sources agree on the state of the evidence).
- **F59.** In the one long trial with paying customers, what changed behaviour was how people paid and how the options were shown. In Gothenburg in 2013–2014, 83 households (195 people) paid a monthly subscription for public transport, car sharing, car rental, taxi and bike sharing through one app for six months: 93% were satisfied and 79% wanted to continue; 42% reported a change of means and 34% a change in how much they plan before a trip. Car owners drove far less than they had expected. The authors trace the effect to paying only for what is used in place of a monthly pass, to a daily ticket that removed zones and time limits, and to an app that listed every option each time, "just the right level of resistance" against habit; they place these "deeper than the general MaaS concept of 'bundling' or having everything in 'one spot'". Participants "were unaware of their preconditions, travel needs, and behavior when joining the service" and corrected their subscription month by month. Source: [AP-019](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-019_stromberg_2018_inviting-travelers-smorgasbord-sustainable.md) (PDF p.6, p.9–14). Leg: whole journey, urban. Confidence: low (one grade B study: self-selected, central, highly educated households in one city, no control group, and the authors' group ran the trial).
- **F76.** The authors of the Sydney trial ended by doubting the premise. Of 91 participants 82% already used both public transport and a car every week; 43% joined to support their employer's initiative and 15% for more streamlined access to transport; asked to rank journey planners they put Google Maps first and the trial app third; afterwards most "could not recall any significant changes in their travelling". The authors wrote in 2021: "a single truly intermodal journey planner is, we believe, yet to exist", journey planners with contactless payment "will keep most travellers happy", and "have we, in promoting MaaS, overestimated what passengers really want?". A Dutch policy institute, as summarised by the Japanese ministry, judged it "unlikely" that such services bring a large change to daily mobility within a few years. Sources: [GR-037](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-037_imove-australia_2021_sydney-maas-trial-final-report.md) (PDF p.137–138, p.184–194, p.198, p.203), [GR-047](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-047_mlit_2025_chiiki-koutsuu-digital-gijutsu-report.md) (PDF p.162, summary of a Dutch study). Leg: whole journey. Confidence: medium (two grade B sources; the second is a summary of another study). It adds to F29 and F58.
- **F31.** What users want first is a service that works. In English focus groups (146 people) shown a regional combined-travel app, the concern named most in cities, suburbs and villages alike was that "presumably no travel app will improve the actual services"; the app is "the icing on an imaginary cake. We need a decent cake". The author concludes that such an app "is a facilitator not a driver of travel behaviour change". Suburban commuters were the most sceptical and the most interested in live information and station parking. Interviews in three other English cities reach the same conclusion (F58), as do the Italian and German surveys (F46) and the app reviews (F47). Sources: [AP-016](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-016_mcilroy_2024_reservation-i-have-that.md) (PDF p.7–8, p.11–13), [AP-004](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-004_alyavina_2020_mobility-as-service-sustainable.md) (PDF p.16–17). Confidence: high (two independent grade A studies agree, supported by F46 and F47).

### What success (Q34–Q40)

**Q34, Q35, Q39. What makes a journey go well, and how would improvement be noticed?** Partial.

- **F46.** Punctuality first. Asked what would make them use public transport more, Italians answer punctuality (41%), then cost (38%) and availability (30%); in the rest of Europe cost comes first. In Germany the obstacles are no or poor connections (more than half the population), travel time (40%) and unreliability (40%), far ahead of unclear fares (15%) and too little information (16%). US riders name frequency and accurate arrival times ahead of fares. Sources: [GR-017](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-017_ipsos-europ-assistance_2025_mobility-barometer.md) (PDF p.20, chart image), [GR-041](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-041_bmv-infas_2025_mobilitaet-in-deutschland-2023.md) (p.104–106), [GR-018](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-018_moovit_2024_global-public-transport-report.md). Confidence: high.
- **F32.** Predictability is what users ask of the journey: "I would rather longer waits and better predictability"; travellers "seek to lower the trip time uncertainty" and want "control over time and travel decisions". Longer trips are less satisfying, and passengers on longer distances are less satisfied (F54). ISTAT titled a section "the range of trips and the uncertainty of their duration are growing". Sources: [OF-004](Sources/Online_Forums/OF-004_hacker-news_2025_hn-i-built-my-own-citymapper.md), [GR-040](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-040_trb_2003_tcrp-92-traveler-information.md) (PDF p.22), [AP-017](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-017_vos_2017_travel-satisfaction-revisited-pivotal.md), [AP-001](Sources/Academic_Papers/AP-001_goransson_2023_factors-that-make-public.md) (PDF p.8), [GR-024](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-024_istat_2018_spostamenti-quotidiani-e-nuove-forme-di-mobilita.md). Confidence: medium.
- Time on board is used and valued by some: rail travellers work or study, and avoid changes to protect it; others rate the possibility low. Confidence: low.

**Q36. Planning a multimodal trip with confidence?** Weak. Signs: wider connections on request (F48), a guaranteed connection (F16), a guaranteed way home ([GR-009](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-009_oecd-itf_2021_integrating-public-transport-into-maas.md)), and availability one can count on at the hub ([GR-012](Sources/Global_Reports/GR-012_uitp_2023_policy-brief-mobility-hubs.md)). Travellers' own answer is to keep one leg under their control and to add a buffer, at the price of the "wait time dilemma" (F9).

**Q37. Handling a disruption calmly?** Partial. What the evidence points to: the reason, stated honestly; a delay estimate that is not announced unless it is fairly sure; alternatives that take the live situation into account; the current trip kept in view; a ticket that follows the new route; compensation without a claim (F20, F42, F43); knowing what a missed connection implies and who answers for it (Q17, F50). The gap between knowing nothing and knowing everything at the start is counted in hours in one simulation (F57).

**Q38. Which decisions do commuters want to keep?** Weak. Users object to tools that decide for them (Q31) and in Sydney to committing a month in advance (F41). In Gothenburg participants accepted more planning and valued being made to choose each time (F59). Nothing direct for commuters.

**Q40. Which problems are worth investigating further?** Not answered here: it is the outcome of the synthesis and Marco's choice. The evidence points most strongly at four areas: the disrupted journey (F20, F42, F43); the hybrid week and what to buy for it (F38 to F41); the change between legs and its buffer (F16, F48, F9); and the daily check of a known trip (F45). The papers read in the evening add a fifth to verify with commuters: the leg from the arrival station to the workplace (F53). The pages read at night point the same way from another side: the first and last legs are where connections are lost and where rules and rights do not reach (F62, F69, F74).

## Decisions taken

- **The file keeps its name and its finding numbers**, so that links from the interview script still work. Findings added after the complete reading start at F33; those added after the 18 papers start at F53. F5, F9, F21, F30 and F31 kept their numbers and were rewritten from the full texts.
- **Confidence** follows the rules in the instructions with the grades in the source files, which do not yet count citations among sources.
- **Reviews** are reported as perceptions. A finding that rests on many independent reviews saying the same thing is rated medium, as the rules allow; one that rests on single reviews is rated low.
- **Sources outside the scope** in context (Japanese rural areas, Korean taxis, US city buses) are used only for mechanisms that transfer, and this is said where they are used.
- **Papers that were not obtained** (AP-002, AP-020) are not used as evidence.
- **Scope widened on 2026-10-08.** No finding was removed or rewritten for it. Marco's figure of 86% of Italians using the car was not found as such in the sources: the nearest figures are 60.8% of trips by car (F12), 84.6% of commuters always using one single means of any kind (F33) and a car in 96% of households in one survey (F64).
- **The forum diaries (OF-002) are used as one person's account.** They are grade C and mostly by one writer, a railway enthusiast on long weekend trips, so F72 to F75 are rated low whatever their detail, and are written as what he reports.
- **Three of the papers read in the evening are outside the scope in context** (Chinese high-speed rail, Dhaka, a US bus network). They are used together for one pattern, the longer leg at the work end (F53), and this is said in the finding.
